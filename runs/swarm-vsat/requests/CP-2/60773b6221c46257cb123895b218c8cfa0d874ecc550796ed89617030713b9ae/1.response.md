## Findings

### Bottom line

Viasat is a leveraged satellite operator whose liquidity covers the next twelve months but whose free cash flow after interest and capital spending, without one-off receipts, is thin against USD 6.6 billion of debt, so creditors depend on ViaSat-3 service and on refinancing.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-K_2026-03-31_vsat-20260331.htm.jsonl, pages 114, 130; calculation C1; calculation C12

Gross debt is 4.49x last-twelve-month EBITDA and net debt 3.28x, but EBITDA is derived from statement lines and the 4.73x cover on cash-paid interest falls to 2.43x before interest capitalized on satellites under construction. Liquidity is USD 2,886.12 million and the 2027 secured notes of USD 600.0 million are already current, yet 49.30% of principal falls due in fiscal 2030. The primary downside path is a slow ViaSat-3 ramp that stalls EBITDA while interest stops being capitalized.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-Q_2026-06-30_vsat-20260630.htm.jsonl, pages 32, 38, 70; calculation C11; calculation C12; calculation C13

### Contrary evidence and what would change the view

The strongest evidence against a thin-cash-flow view is operating momentum and a capital programme that is ending. Communication services segment operating profit rose 52.73% in the first quarter of fiscal 2027 with the margin at 8% against 5%, aviation services grew 10.75%, management says capital spending may be flat to slightly down, firm backlog is USD 4,217.7 million, and Ligado still owes USD 100 million plus quarterly payments of about USD 16 million. If ViaSat-3 service adds revenue while capital spending falls, free cash flow without one-offs could rise well above USD 158.29 million, and the September 2024 refinancing suggests lenders will still lend.

### Limitations

- No covenant level is printed for the maximum leverage and minimum cover tests on three facilities, so the liquidity and leverage conclusions cannot be tied to headroom and a covenant-driven acceleration cannot be ruled in or out.
- No credit rating, traded price or spread is supplied and the issuer's fair value estimates of each note series are rounded model values, so refinancing capacity rests on past issuance and those estimates, not on current market access or cost, and the refinancing conclusion could be too benign if spreads have widened.
- Maintenance and growth capital spending are not split, so the capital programme's flexibility cannot be sized and the free cash flow conclusion assumes the indicated flat-to-slightly-down spending is achievable.
- Contract lengths, churn and renewal terms are not disclosed and recurring in-flight contracts carry no minimum purchase requirement, so the revenue durability judgement rests on growth rates and installed base, not on contracted minimums.
- The latest balance sheet is 128 days before the as-of date, so liquidity, the 2027 secured notes and the ViaSat-3 service starts may differ today and the near-term liquidity view is a snapshot.
- Earnings and cash cannot be split between the Viasat and Inmarsat borrowing groups, so each group's own ability to service its debt is not established and the combined ratios may hide a weaker group.

### Company description

Viasat sells connectivity services to airlines, ships, governments and fixed users, and defense, encryption and tactical networking products, through communication services (71.11% of fiscal 2026 revenue) and defense and advanced technologies (28.89%). Services were 70.57% of revenue, which gives recurring visibility, while almost all contracts are fixed-price, so cost overruns fall on Viasat. The May 2023 Inmarsat acquisition doubled the satellite and debt base and added L-band safety spectrum and customer relationships, and the group has since sold the Link-16 business, the energy services integration business and Navarino UK. These facts support cash-flow visibility in aviation and government satcom but raise debt service needs, so refinancing confidence depends on converting the satellite investment into EBITDA.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-K_2026-03-31_vsat-20260331.htm.jsonl, pages 6, 92, 169, 205; calculation C1

### Ownership and group structure

Viasat is a listed, founder-led company with no sponsor or parent identified, so there is no sponsor behaviour to assess, and the filings document no dividend, no debt-funded distribution and a fall in principal. Debt sits in two groups: the Inmarsat group's term loan and 9.00% secured notes on their own collateral, and Viasat's secured term loans, 2027 secured notes and export-credit loan, with unsecured 2028 and 2031 notes ranking behind both and no subsidiary guaranteeing Viasat's debt at March 31, 2026. TrellisWare is majority-owned, so cash it distributes partly leaks to minority holders. The implication is that creditors rely on group-level cash flow, structural position by instrument and unseen holders' behaviour, all of which need the proxy statement, the executed documents and the cooperation agreement to confirm.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-K_2026-03-31_vsat-20260331.htm.jsonl, pages 147, 181, 183, 186; 10-Q_2026-06-30_vsat-20260630.htm.jsonl, page 32

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Volume drives revenue: installed commercial aircraft grew 9.46% year on year, while about 130,000 U.S. fixed subscribers at USD 113 per month became about 115,000 at USD 111 between March and June 2026, a fall of 11.54% in the count, because bandwidth is being allocated to aviation. Services were 70.57% of revenue and the U.S. Government 16%, with the five largest contracts about 19%; defense products are lumpy and depend on awards and government funding, and substantially all U.S. Government backlog can be terminated for convenience. Pricing power is not disclosed and contract lengths, churn and renewals are not stated; recurring in-flight service contracts carry no minimum service purchase requirements and are outside the USD 4.2 billion of remaining performance obligations, which refines the earlier finding that no airline contract term is disclosed. Revenue is therefore recurring and growing in aviation and government satcom, cyclical with airline traffic and defense budgets, concentrated at the top contracts, exposed to substitution by low earth orbit services such as Starlink, and structurally declining in U.S. fixed broadband, which limits cash-flow visibility to backlog and installed base.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Cost of service revenues was 46% of fiscal 2026 revenue, cost of product revenues 21%, selling, general and administrative 22%, independent research and development 4% and amortization of acquired intangibles 6%, with depreciation of USD 1,038.515 million equal to 22.38% of revenue, so the cost base is heavily fixed in satellites and ground networks. EBITDA margin was 31.52% in fiscal 2026 and 31.03% over the last twelve months; communication services segment margin improved from 5% to 8% in the first quarter while defense margin eased from 21% to 15% on a mix with less licensing revenue. Margins can be defended under modest volume pressure because management says capital spending may be flat to slightly down and selling cost fell, but fixed-price contracts, higher research spending (up 33% in the quarter) and fixed satellite depreciation leave little room if revenue stalls, so operating leverage cuts both ways.

### Competitive forces

Rivalry is intense and rising: the annual report names SpaceX, SES, Gogo, Panasonic Avionics, Anuvu, Iridium, Thales and Amazon Leo in aviation, which can force price or service concessions and shorten contract terms. New entrants are low earth orbit constellations, and the annual report says the geostationary industry is being disrupted by their adoption, which threatens renewals in aviation and maritime. Substitutes include air-to-ground and cellular services in fixed and mobility markets. Buyer power is high with airlines and governments, and the largest customer is the U.S. Government. Supplier power is moderate: components come from sole-source or limited suppliers and capacity is bought from third parties, which exposes delivery and cost. The credit implication is that margin durability and retention depend on ViaSat-3 capacity delivering cost per bit and on L-band safety services that LEO does not replace.
Basis: 10-K_2026-03-31_vsat-20260331.htm.jsonl, pages 26, 48, 61; Business & governance (CP-1A)

### Regulatory and macro factors

Regulation is material because authorizations to operate satellites and earth stations are conditional on milestones and fees, and loss or delay could stop service, while export controls, sanctions and U.S. Government contract audit rules apply; this bears on revenue and on orbital and spectrum assets recorded at USD 1,080.0 million in the Inmarsat purchase accounting. Economic factors matter through airline traffic and defense budgets, and 31.88% of revenue is from non-U.S. customers though business is generally conducted in U.S. dollars. Interest rates matter because 38.70% of principal is floating. Social factors do not appear to be a primary credit driver on the materials supplied.

### Outlook, tailwinds and headwinds

Over twelve to twenty-four months the commercial start of ViaSat-3 service on the second and third satellites, lower capital spending, a lower interest burden from repaid debt and any extension of the Inmarsat revolving facility are the main tailwinds; the 2027 secured notes, the revolver expiry, the end of interest capitalization on satellites entering service and the loss of one-off Ligado and Navarino receipts are headwinds that pressure free cash flow and refinancing. Over three to five years the tailwinds are growth in aircraft and vessels needing connectivity, government demand for resilient communications and seven further satellites completing, while the headwinds are the fiscal 2030 maturities, low earth orbit competition at contract renewals and further satellite capital needs. Sector headwinds are the LEO disruption and tariffs affecting supply; sector tailwinds are rising connectivity demand. Company tailwinds are firm backlog and the Ligado stream of about USD 16 million a quarter.

### Downside scenario

The initiating event is a slower or weaker ViaSat-3 ramp, possible given that the first satellite recovered less than 10% of its throughput and that service starts are unconfirmed after August 6, 2026. The first operational break is that capacity stays rationed, so fixed services keep shrinking (down 27.08% in the first quarter) and aviation growth slows. Revenue and EBITDA stall near last-twelve-month levels while interest on satellites entering service stops being capitalized, so reported cover falls toward 2.43x; each 10% fall in EBITDA lifts gross leverage to 4.99x. Free cash flow without one-offs stays near USD 158.29 million, so the USD 600.0 million of 2027 secured notes and the Inmarsat revolver must be refinanced rather than repaid. Management levers are capital spending cuts, disposals such as Navarino, TrellisWare dividends, drawing the revolvers and collecting Ligado. Cash of USD 1,735.819 million covers the current portion 2.62 times, so resilience appears sufficient to avoid liquidity distress inside twelve months but not to repay the fiscal 2030 maturities without market access. Primary downside path: a slow ViaSat-3 ramp stalls EBITDA while interest stops being capitalized and the 2027 and fiscal 2030 maturities need refinancing.

### Overall credit view
