## Findings

### Bottom line

Fundamental credit quality is adequate but weakening: inventory, equity and a long bond ladder support creditors, while earnings, margins and cash conversion have deteriorated enough that leverage has more than doubled in two years and now leans on a revolver that is increasingly drawn.

The primary downside path is further price and incentive cuts that compress margin while inventory and acquisitions keep absorbing revolver capacity; company guidance already implies a fourth quarter well below last year, so this module treats cash conversion, and not asset cover, as the measure that decides the view.

On the issuer own measure, net homebuilding debt to net capital was 34.9%, against 25.9% at FY2025.

### Contrary evidence and what would change the view

The strongest case for a better view is that the balance sheet is built for this part of the cycle. Inventories cover total debt 2.07x, equity covers it 1.41x, covenants are reported as met, new lenders joined the enlarged revolver, the land pipeline was cut 24.5% in 2025 so land spend can fall, fixed cost was reduced through an 11.4% headcount cut, and the fourth quarter of 2025 turned a nine-month operating outflow into a full-year inflow. The view would improve if orders and prices stabilise so that gross margin holds at or above the 17.0% nine-month level, if fourth-quarter operating cash flow matches or beats the prior year, and if the HistoryMaker purchase proves funded from cash flow. It would worsen if the fourth quarter falls short of the guidance-implied revenue, if revolver utilisation rises above 38.00% with no seasonal reversal, if impairments spread beyond the six communities written down in the third quarter, or if repurchases continue while operating cash flow stays negative.

### Company description and credit linkage

Century Communities builds and sells homes, and home sales are 95.35% of revenue, with mortgage services and Colorado multi-family rentals as small additions. Revenue is transactional, not contracted: backlog covers only 1.30 months of last-twelve-months revenue, so cash flow depends on orders converting at current prices, and the affordable entry-level focus gives volume but limited pricing power. Cash-flow visibility is therefore low, debt service depends on liquidity and asset sales rather than on contracted receipts, and refinancing confidence rests on inventory cover.

### Ownership and group structure

The company is public with founder-managers holding 13.9%, no sponsor and no parent. The notes are guaranteed by substantially all wholly-owned operating subsidiaries, while the financial services subsidiaries and their mortgage repurchase facilities sit outside the guarantee group and the parent guarantees portions of the multi-family construction loans. Creditors therefore rank behind secured construction and warehouse lenders on their assets, and governance risk is medium because payments to shareholders and acquisitions are discretionary and board independence is not established.

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Revenue is cyclical, discretionary and sensitive to mortgage rates. Nine-month deliveries fell 5.38% and average price fell 7.13%, and in the third quarter price fell 10.20% against deliveries down only 1.77%, so price is now the main driver. Orders equalled 1.05x deliveries over nine months and backlog value is flat at USD 413.96 million, which supports near-term volume but not price, and cancellation risk rises with rates because contracts carry financing contingencies. Weak pricing power means revenue declines flow through to margin and cash.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Margins are hard to defend under falling prices. Selling, general and administrative cost was 14.64% of home sales revenue in nine months against 13.12% a year earlier because it fell only 1.94%, direct construction costs have fallen but incentives and impairments offset them, and interest charged through cost of sales is added back in EBITDA. Fixed overhead and incentives make operating leverage the transmission path from price declines to lower EBITDA, which weakens interest cover and leverage together.

### Industry forces

Rivalry is intense and barriers are low: homebuilders compete for buyers, land, labour and financing, and recent industry consolidation changes the competitive balance. Buyers hold power through mortgage affordability and incentives, substitutes include existing homes, foreclosures and rentals, and suppliers and subcontractors are not tied by long contracts, with tariffs on cabinets and lumber a cost risk. Each force acts through price and incentive levels, which feed margin, impairment and cash flow, so none is a direct default driver but all raise the sensitivity of earnings to the housing cycle.

### Macro and policy factors

Macro and policy factors are material. Rates feed demand and cancellations, tariffs feed cost, and both pressure margin and orders without a clear date for relief.

### Credit-translated strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats

Strengths are asset cover, the maturity ladder, liquidity, scale and a flexible land model. Weaknesses are leverage, cash conversion, margin and the cost base. Opportunities are a rate decline that revives orders and the added Dallas scale if integration is cheap, both of which would improve cash generation and leverage. Threats are further rate rises, tariff costs, more impairments and a debt-funded acquisition pattern, each of which would reduce liquidity or raise leverage.

### Financial profile and credit quality

Scale is strong and competitive advantage is average, with a low-cost entry-level offer but no pricing moat. Diversification is average across five regions but one product type. ### Downside scenario

A realistic downside starts with mortgage rates staying high through the winter, so orders slow and incentives rise again. The first operational break is lower average price and weaker backlog conversion, which cuts revenue and gross margin while overhead stays fixed, as it did in 2026. Operating cash flow then stays negative even after the seasonal reversal, so the revolver stays drawn and undrawn capacity of USD 744.0 million falls; if the HistoryMaker purchase was paid from the revolver, which the filings do not say, capacity would be USD 583.8 million. Management levers are slower land spend, no repurchases and lower overhead. Resilience appears sufficient to avoid liquidity distress within 12 months because no bond matures before 2029 and the revolver is extended to 2030, but leverage would rise and covenant headroom is not known. The observable triggers are fourth-quarter operating cash flow, revolver utilisation and gross margin.

### Overall credit view

Overall, Century Communities presents an adequate business profile supported by scale, asset cover and a long bond ladder, but credit quality is constrained by falling margins, negative cash conversion and rising leverage. Cash-flow durability appears weak at present because price declines flow through a fixed cost base into a working-capital build financed on the revolver. Refinancing capacity is likely to depend on the fourth-quarter cash reversal and on keeping the revolver available until the 2029 notes. The primary downside path is price and incentive pressure that holds margin low while borrowing rises. Further analysis would require covenant levels, HistoryMaker earnings and funding, and holders above 5%. Fundamental credit analysis completed. Top risk: cash conversion.
