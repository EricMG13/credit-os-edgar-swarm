### Company description and business model

Diversified Healthcare Trust is a Maryland healthcare REIT that earns income in two ways. Managed senior living communities are leased to its own taxable subsidiaries and run by third-party managers for a fee, and medical office and life science properties are let to tenants under net, modified gross and full-service leases; a small book of triple net leased senior living and wellness properties adds contractual rent. Revenue in the larger segment is residents' fees that move with occupancy and rate, so cash flow visibility is lower than for a net lease REIT and operating leverage is high. The portfolio changed in 2025, when 69 properties were sold and the medical office base shrank; senior housing now produces 87.01% of revenue and 61.5% of net operating income, which ties debt service to operator performance in one segment.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-K_2025-12-31_dhc.htm.jsonl, pages 7, 10–11; 10-Q_2026-09-30_dhc.htm.jsonl, pages 20, 30; calculation M7

### Ownership and group structure

The trust is a public REIT with no sponsor fund and no equity cure behind it. ABP Trust and the chair hold 9.8% of the shares and control the parent of the external manager, which employs every officer and holds agreements to 2045 with a termination fee of 19 years to 20 years of fees, so governance leverage for creditors rests on independent Trustees. Debt sits at the parent and at property-owning subsidiaries: the 4.375% notes due 2031 carry unsecured subsidiary guarantees, the 7.25% notes due 2030 are secured on the equity of subsidiaries owning 36 properties, and the other three unsecured series carry no guarantee, so they rank behind USD 841.40 million of secured principal and behind the creditors of subsidiaries. Financial policy has been conservative in conduct: small distributions, no acquisitions in 2023, 2024 or 2025, and property sales applied to debt; detailed ranking and legal capacity belong to Structural priority and Legal & covenants, for which the debt documents are not supplied.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-Q_2026-09-30_dhc.htm.jsonl, pages 13–15, 26, 50–51; 10-K_2025-12-31_dhc.htm.jsonl, pages 124–125, 195, 213

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Residents' fees are 91.11% basic housing and support services paid privately and 6.40% from Medicare and Medicaid, so volume and rate drive revenue and government reimbursement is a small exposure. Occupancy across all senior housing properties was 83.8% in Q3 2026 against 81.5% a year earlier and the average monthly rate was USD 5,670 against USD 5,472, a rise of 3.62%, so rate has carried part of the gain. The trust cites constrained supply, with inventory growth of 0.5% and absorption of 2.8% in the fourth quarter of 2025 from a third-party source, which supports occupancy but is a management reading. Medical office and life science revenue is contractual but shrinking, at USD 40.40 million against USD 48.20 million in Q3, with 20.3% of annualized rental income expiring in 2028 and a weighted average remaining term of 4.9 years; new and renewal leases in Q3 2026 were signed at a rental rate change of 25.2%. Revenue is recurring in form, cyclical in occupancy and concentrated in one segment, and the filings describe no long-term contract protection for resident revenue.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Costs follow the operation of the communities and sit with the managers: labor and benefits were 48.18% of segment revenue in Q3 2026 against 51.72% a year earlier and management fees 5.68% against 5.31%, with insurance, utilities and food the other named pressures. Net operating income margin rose to 15.52% from 8.88% and part of that gain reflects a smaller portfolio, since units fell from 24,906 to 22,445, so the margin is both an improvement and a base for operating deleverage. Management fees move with revenue and incentive fees share the upside above target EBITDA, so lenders bear the downside. The issuer expects labor, insurance and food cost increases to moderate, which is a management view; margins can be defended under modest volume pressure but not under a combined wage and occupancy shock.

### Porter's Five Forces

Competitive rivalry matters through occupancy: senior housing competes community by community, and supply growth is low at 0.5% a year in the trust's cited data, which limits the pressure on occupancy and rate. The threat of new entrants is restrained by the cost of financing and construction, which the annual report names as the reason supply has stayed low, so competitors cannot quickly add units and dilute pricing. Buyer power is moderate: residents pay mostly privately and monthly rates rose 3.62% in a year, while medical office tenants renewed at higher rents, so there is no evidence of pricing pressure; the threat of substitutes, such as home care, is not established in the sources. Supplier power is the weaker side: labor is the largest cost line at 48.18% of segment revenue and the trust relies on managers that set rents and hire staff, so wage inflation and manager performance are the forces that most affect margin and cash flow.

### PEST

PEST factors are only partly a primary credit driver. Regulatory exposure is small, because Medicare and Medicaid are 6.40% of residents' fees and only a small amount of net operating income comes from properties that depend mainly on them; economic factors matter more, through labor inflation, interest rates and the financing market that sets the cost of refinancing the 2028 maturities; the social factor, a growing population aged 75 and over at about 4% a year between 2025 and 2035 on the trust's cited census projection, supports demand; technological factors do not appear to be a primary credit driver in the materials provided.

### Qualitative downside scenario
