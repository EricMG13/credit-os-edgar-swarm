## Findings

### Bottom line

Millicom presents an adequate credit profile, with strong margins and a larger operating base, but creditors carry leverage that rose with acquisitions, dividends that absorb most free cash flow and earnings concentrated in two countries.
Basis: calculation C01; calculation C03; calculation C05; Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A)

Liquidity rests on a revolving facility whose availability after 2025 is not established, and no debt documents were supplied, so protections for creditors are not established.

### Company description and credit linkage

Millicom sells mobile and fixed services under the Tigo brand in eleven Latin American markets, and mobile generated 60% of 2025 service revenue with fixed and other services at 38%. Postpaid and fixed subscriptions give recurring monthly revenue, whereas prepaid customers buy packages that expire after days to weeks, so the postpaid share, 30.79% of mobile customers, matters for cash-flow visibility. Most towers were sold and leased back, which cut capital intensity but converted it into 15-year lease obligations, so lease payments were USD 483 million in nine months 2026, and Coltel added a large share of the mobile customer base in 2026. The consequence is a business with recurring revenue and heavy fixed charges, where debt service capacity depends on keeping the postpaid base and the margin.

### Ownership and group structure

Atlas Investissement, ultimately owned by the Niel family, holds 42.2% of the voting shares and four of eight directors are affiliated with it, so one holder can influence director elections and corporate actions without owning a majority. The Luxembourg parent depends on subsidiaries to send it cash, and about 70% of gross debt sits at the operating entities with the other 30% at corporate level, so parent creditors rank behind operating-company lenders except where the parent guarantees that debt, and guarantees and legal rank are not established. The Honduras joint venture, in which Millicom holds 66.67%, carries its own debt without recourse to the parent. The consequence is that cash reaching parent creditors depends on dividends and repatriation from subsidiaries, with USD 79 million repatriated from joint ventures and associates in nine months 2026, and on board decisions influenced by the largest shareholder.

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Revenue is predictable in postpaid, fixed and business-to-business lines and cyclical in prepaid and equipment sales. Organic service revenue grew 5.3% in nine months 2026, helped by a more-for-more pricing strategy that raised mobile ARPU 19.0% in dollars, including currency effects, to USD 7.4 per month excluding Coltel, and postpaid customers excluding acquisitions grew. Fixed revenue is weaker underneath: excluding acquisitions the fixed revenue generating units would have declined because fixed telephony is shrinking, and home ARPU rose to USD 27.7 per month while the base would be shrinking without acquisitions. Competition on price and customer experience in markets with penetration above 100% limits further volume growth, so pricing power is moderate and the revenue outlook rests on mix migration rather than subscriber growth.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

### Competitive forces

Rivalry is intense: the annual report names América Móvil, Telefónica and Liberty Latin America as main competitors with greater resources, adds WOM in Colombia, where competitors may hold a dominant position, and describes telecom services as largely commoditized, so competition on price can compress margin and raise churn. Entry is limited by the need for spectrum licences with fixed terms, which is an analyst inference, and several licences expire from 2026. Substitution is a risk mainly for pay-TV through wireless and satellite alternatives and for fixed telephony. Buyers have little to hold them in prepaid, where no contract is signed, but credit exposure to certain business customers, mainly governments, is concentrated. Suppliers matter through spectrum fees, the tower leaseback and programming content such as the World Cup. The consequence is that pricing power is clearest in Guatemala, where ARPU rose and margin is highest, and weakest in Colombia, the largest market.

### Outlook, tailwinds and headwinds

### Downside scenario

The most plausible downside is a price war and asymmetric regulation in Colombia during integration, coinciding with a reversal of local currencies. The operating break comes first in Colombian ARPU and postpaid growth, where a competitor with a dominant position could cut prices. Revenue then grows below the 5.3% organic rate and Adjusted EBITDA margin falls because network and integration costs do not flex, while lease payments and finance charges of USD 915 million in nine months remain fixed claims on the cash that is left. Management can cut or defer dividends that are not yet declared, defer capital expenditure, sell the mobile financial services business and draw development-bank funding, and market access in 2026 shows these routes are open. Resilience therefore appears sufficient to avoid liquidity distress within twelve months if refinancing stays available, but not if the revolving facility is drawn, because the cushion would then be gone. The observable triggers are Colombian service revenue growth, the margin, free cash flow against dividends and the refinancing of the next two years of maturities.

### Overall credit view

Overall, Millicom presents an adequate business profile supported by high margins, scale in eleven markets and a rising postpaid base, but credit quality is constrained by acquisition-driven leverage, dividends that absorb 0.87x of equity free cash flow and earnings concentrated in Guatemala and Colombia. The primary downside path is a Colombian price war and currency reversal that cut earnings while leases, interest and dividends continue.
