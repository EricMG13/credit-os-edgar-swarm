## Findings

Chemours is a stretched but liquid credit: leverage is high and fell in 2026 only through an asset sale, while PFAS settlements, the 2028 maturity wall and a normalising refrigerant business leave little margin for error.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C2

### Company description

Chemours earns its cash from three product families sold into regulation-driven and cyclical markets: refrigerants and thermal solutions (Thermal & Specialized Solutions), titanium dioxide pigment (Titanium Technologies) and fluoropolymer materials (Advanced Performance Materials). In fiscal 2025 they were 35.57%, 41.82% and 21.75% of net sales of USD 5,808 million, and in the nine months to September 30, 2026 36.99%, 42.09% and 20.08%, so the sales mix is balanced but earnings are not. The business supports cash-flow visibility only in part: refrigerant sales follow a regulatory transition and a seasonal cooling season, titanium dioxide follows construction and packaging demand, and the company's own legacy-site liabilities attach to plants that remain in operation, which limits liquidity preservation and keeps refinancing confidence tied to earnings recovery.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-K_2025-12-31_cc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 6; calculation C15

### Ownership and group structure

Chemours is a widely held public company with no controlling shareholder or sponsor identified, so there is no owner able to extract cash or to inject equity, and the proxy statement that would show large holders and board independence was not supplied. Debt sits at the parent and is guaranteed by domestic subsidiaries; the last guarantor-group summary covered fiscal 2025 and showed obligor net sales of 67.99% of group net sales, so at least 32.01% is earned outside the obligor group, a floor because intercompany sales inflate the obligor total. The 2034 notes carry a guarantee from a guarantor that no filing names, so the current perimeter is not established, and receivables are sold through a non-guarantor special purpose entity, so Structural priority must settle how claims rank.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-K_2025-12-31_cc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 114; calculation C21

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Revenue is cyclical and policy-driven rather than recurring. Opteon sales jumped in 2025 on stationary air conditioning volumes tied to the AIM Act and fell in 2026 as the initial channel fill faded; nine-month net sales were flat as a 3% price gain offset a 3% volume fall, and Titanium Technologies lifted price 10% in the third quarter while volume rose 1%. Pricing power is real where regulation or quality limits substitutes, but the Opteon composition patents expire through the end of the decade and titanium dioxide demand is about 73.00% of nameplate capacity, so price gains depend on discipline rather than structure. Pigment grades are not fully substitutable because of quality consistency and specialty fluoropolymers are chosen for performance in demanding uses, but the filings give no retention or contract-duration data, so switching costs are not established; one pigment customer takes more than 20% of segment sales, which concentrates revenue risk in the segment meant to offset refrigerants.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Margins are defended poorly under volume pressure. Cost of goods sold rose 4% in the third quarter on higher fixed costs and lower production rates while sales rose 3%, gross margin for nine months fell 0.50 percentage points to 16.16%, and Thermal & Specialized Solutions margin dropped to 27% from 35%; Titanium Technologies margin recovered to 13% from 4% on price. Energy is about 10% of titanium dioxide production cost and the company buys its titanium ore, chlorine and calcined petroleum coke from outside suppliers, with its own mines supplying under 10% of ore needs, so input shocks pass through unless priced. ### Competitive forces

Rivalry is intense where capacity exceeds demand and moderate where patents protect. Refrigerant competitors include Solstice Advanced Materials, Arkema, Orbia, Daikin and Chinese producers, titanium dioxide competitors include Tronox, LB Group, Kronos and INEOS, and titanium dioxide demand is 73.00% of nameplate capacity, which caps price. New entry is limited by chloride process know-how, patents to the end of the decade for the main Opteon molecule and regulatory product approvals; substitutes are regulatory (the shift from older refrigerants) rather than competing products, and pigment grades are not fully substitutable. Buyers are fragmented at group level but concentrated in pigment, where the ten largest customers take more than 44% of sales, and supplier power is moderate: titanium ore comes from several suppliers on contracts and can be mixed across sites, chlorine and energy follow regional price cycles, and fluorspar and chlorinated organics are sourced from many suppliers. For creditors this means refrigerant earnings are protected only until patents lapse and pigment earnings depend on price discipline.

### Qualitative downside scenario

The scenario starts with titanium dioxide price gains fading because demand stays near 73.00% of capacity while refrigerant aftermarket volume keeps falling. The first operational break is lower volume at the refrigerant plants and weaker price realisation in pigment, which lifts unit cost because the cost base is largely fixed. ### Overall credit view

Overall, Chemours presents an adequate but concentrated business profile supported by leading refrigerant and pigment positions, near-term liquidity that covers the 2026 and 2027 payments and cost sharing for legacy liabilities, but credit quality is constrained by high leverage, thin interest cover and unquantified PFAS exposure.
