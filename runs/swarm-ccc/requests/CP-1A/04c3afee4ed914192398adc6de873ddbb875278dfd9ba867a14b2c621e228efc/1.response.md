## Findings

### Bottom line

CCC is a recurring-revenue software platform with a broad customer base, but its pending sale to a consortium led by GTCR leaves the senior secured term loan's treatment unstated, which is the one fact that could change creditor protection. Software subscriptions are 95.85% of nine-month 2026 revenue, no customer exceeded 10% of 2025 revenue, and contracted revenue due within a year equals 70.75% of last-twelve-months revenue, so the operating base supports debt service.

Governance gives creditors limited visibility. The filings name no controlling holder, the finance and technology chiefs are interim, and the proxy statement that would carry ownership and board detail is not supplied. The documented pattern is shareholder-favouring and partly debt-funded: in 2025 the company borrowed USD 525.0 million of new term loans and repurchased USD 600.57 million of its shares.

### Key findings

The operating model supports debt service because revenue is overwhelmingly subscription-based and spread across a broad customer base. Software subscriptions grew 10.11% in the first nine months of 2026, about 8% of the 10.55% revenue growth came from existing customers and about 2% from new ones, no customer exceeded 10% of revenue in any of the last three years, and software net dollar retention was 108% in the third quarter against gross retention of 98%, so existing customers expand more than they leave. Contracted revenue of USD 1,808 million is 1.59x last-twelve-months revenue, but it is USD 83 million lower than at year-end.

Margin gains are real but partly non-cash, so cash earnings improved by less than the margin suggests. Operating income rose to 17.86% of revenue from 5.61% in the first nine months of 2026, but 47.62% of the rise in operating income came from lower stock compensation, a non-cash cost, with the rest from revenue growth outpacing cost growth.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_ccc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 38; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ccc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 39; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ccc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 40; calculation C06; calculation C20

Customer concentration is low but end-market concentration is high, so the main business risk is a shift in United States auto-claims activity or in how insurers buy technology, not the loss of one customer. Nearly all revenue is earned in the United States, at 99.40% of nine-month 2026 revenue; the company names insurers' in-house systems and large horizontal technology providers as competitors; and part of revenue depends on claim volumes rather than subscriptions. The filings do not split revenue by insurer, repairer or other solution, so exposure by customer group is not established.

The pending take-private is the dominant creditor event, and the filings do not say what happens to the existing debt. The buyers agreed to pay $7.75 per share, an enterprise value of about $6.1bn that is 12.58x last-twelve-months issuer-adjusted EBITDA and puts the term loan principal at 21.00% of that value. That cushion is large at the offer price, but new buyer debt would use it, and the filings refer to the buyer's equity, debt or preferred equity financing without stating amounts, ranking or which entity borrows.

### Contrary evidence and what would change the view

The strongest case against a cautious governance view is that the buyers are paying a price that leaves a thin debt load relative to value: the term loan is 21.00% of enterprise value, the offer is 28.39% above the March 2026 repurchase price, and the credit agreement, which is not supplied, may require repayment on a change of control, which would take lenders out at par. Related-party dealings are small at 0.52% of 2025 revenue, the company states no plans to pay dividends, and revolver borrowings stayed below the 35% usage level that triggers the financial covenant at each 2026 quarter-end.
Basis: EX-99.1_2026-10-29_ccc-ex99_1.htm.jsonl, page 2; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ccc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 22; 10-K_2025-12-31_ccc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 175; 10-K_2025-12-31_ccc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 91; 10-Q_2026-03-31_ccc-20260331.htm.jsonl, page 20; 10-Q_2026-06-30_ccc-20260630.htm.jsonl, page 18; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ccc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 18; calculation C05; calculation C08; calculation C16

The strongest case against the business view is that contracted revenue fell USD 83 million in nine months while revenue grew, that insurers' own systems and artificial-intelligence-led competitors are named threats, and that guidance and the earnings call were withdrawn just as the sale was announced, removing management's forward view. If the merger agreement or the credit agreement shows the term loan repaid at par, or kept with unchanged covenants and no new priority debt, governance risk would fall. Buyer debt placed at the borrower, a consent or amendment that loosens restrictions, or a failed closing that triggers the $149.52 million fee would raise it, and the 2026 proxy statement naming the principal equity owner would show who controls the vote.

### Transaction summary and new debt issues

The company agreed on October 14, 2026 to a cash merger with Mozart Parent, LLC, under which a merger subsidiary merges into the company and the company survives as a wholly owned subsidiary of the buyer. Shareholders receive $7.75 per share. The third-quarter earnings release describes the buyers as a consortium of private investment firms led by GTCR and including Elliott Investment Management, and puts the enterprise value at about $6.1bn, while the quarterly report names only the Mozart entities. Closing is expected in the first quarter of 2027, subject to adoption by holders of a majority of the shares, expiry of the antitrust waiting period and the absence of a blocking order; the agreement has no financing condition and either side may terminate if the merger is not completed by April 14, 2027.

Termination fees run both ways, $149.52 million payable by the company in some circumstances, including a superior proposal, and about $313.99 million payable by the buyer if it fails to fund. Until closing the company has agreed not to incur certain debt, pay dividends or repurchase its shares without the buyer's consent.
