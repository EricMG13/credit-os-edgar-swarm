## Findings

### Bottom line

Fundamental credit is adequate but weakening: contractual option income covers interest comfortably today, yet debt has risen to 4.95x EBITDA and 36.33% of capitalization, above management's own 33% maximum target, while one builder group still supplies 69% of revenue.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C3

The contracts let Lennar pause for up to six months at half the option rate, which would cut last-twelve-months cover of senior note coupons from 3.50x to about 2.75x, before floating-rate interest. Creditors should treat the profile as adequate only while takedowns continue and leverage stops rising.

### Contrary evidence and what would change the view

The strongest evidence against concern is that the structure has performed. No option agreement has been terminated, forfeited or expired since inception, no counterparty delinquency has been recorded, the Lennar Corporation guarantee backs Lennar's payment obligations, and management reports that AFFO has risen for six consecutive quarters. Management states a portfolio yield of 9.3% against an average coupon of 6.43% on the senior notes outstanding after October 6, 2026, the portfolio's weighted average remaining life is 3.3 years against debt that is mostly due from 2030, and a quarterly income run rate of about USD 221 million is about USD 10 million above reported third-quarter revenue, so leverage could ease quickly if the new assets earn as stated.

The view would improve if debt to capitalization returned to 33% or below without new equity because takedown proceeds repay the revolving facility instead of funding new commitments, if fourth-quarter revenue reached the run rate, if covenant levels proved to leave wide headroom, and if the class action were dismissed. It would worsen on any Lennar pause designation or pool-level termination, on a revolving draw that funds a distribution or a repurchase, on revenue below the run rate for a second quarter, or on evidence that the pricing grid or covenants bind at the current leverage.

### Company description and credit linkage

Millrose is a residential land bank organised as a real estate investment trust. It buys and develops land, then gives homebuilders exclusive options to buy finished homesites at set prices on set takedown schedules, and earns monthly option fees on the capital it has invested; it operates in the United States only, in one reportable segment, and was spun off by Lennar on February 7, 2025. Cash flow is therefore a contractual yield on invested capital plus the return of that capital as homesites are taken down, which supports visibility of debt service while the options are performed, and which makes liquidity depend on takedown timing, not on sales of homes.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 10–11, 40, 47; 10-K_2025-12-31_ck0002017206-20251231.htm.jsonl, pages 25, 33

### Ownership and group structure

Class A stock is publicly listed, Class B stock held by the Miller Family carries about 43% of the votes, Lennar holds a de minimis stake since November 28, 2025 but retains Founder's Rights, and Kennedy Lewis runs the company as external manager under a management agreement. The senior notes and credit facilities are guaranteed by two subsidiaries, and the notes are structurally subordinated to the debt of subsidiaries that do not guarantee them, while the rental facility and the purchase money mortgages sit at property-level borrowers with their own collateral. For creditors this means control sits with parties whose interests are not those of lenders, while the guarantors and the unsecured structure put the notes and bank facilities on the same rank.

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Revenue is the product of invested capital and a weighted yield, so it grows with funded capital, not with home prices or volumes. Capital invested reached USD 9,245.4 million at September 30, 2026 at a weighted yield of 9.3%, and takedown proceeds of USD 813.681 million in the quarter compare with USD 1,260.082 million of new funding. Pricing power is limited: the 2024 annual report fixed the rate on the assets transferred at spin-off at 8.5%, set the rate on Lennar's future assets from a three-year BBB index plus 4.00% and a rating adjustment tied to Lennar's credit rating, bounded between 7.0% and 10.0%, and gives Lennar the right to match any lower rate Millrose accepts from another counterparty. The 2025 annual report no longer prints the rating adjustment, so whether it still applies is not established. Other counterparties pay a higher stated yield of 10.8%, and contracted option fees of about USD 3.1 billion at the 2025 year end, excluding new homesites, give visibility of several years of income.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Operating costs are mainly the management fee of 1.25% of Tangible Assets, USD 30.264 million in the third quarter, with stock-based compensation and credit-loss provisions small beside it, which gave an income-from-operations margin of 85.52% in the quarter. The fee follows assets, so margin holds while income grows with assets but falls quickly when income stops growing, because the fee does not fall with a pause, and extraordinary litigation and rating agency fees are borne outside the fee. Interest expense of USD 47.093 million in the quarter is the larger fixed claim and absorbs 26.10% of third-quarter income from operations.

### Capital intensity and cash conversion

The company reports no maintenance or growth capital expenditure; land and development spending is investment in homesites under option, USD 3,150.232 million in the nine months, against takedown proceeds that return capital. Earnings convert to cash well, with operating cash flow excluding takedowns of USD 361.638 million against net income of USD 378.767 million, although development loan income, 0.72% of third-quarter revenue, is paid in kind. Dividends of USD 378.522 million take more than all of it, and the investment programme is financed by net borrowing. ### Competitive forces

Rivalry is limited but real: the 2025 annual report describes a niche of a select number of private funds and asset managers, and warns of aggressive pricing and alternative structures, so the 9.3% yield could compress on new projects, which lowers cover. The threat of new entrants is moderated by operating complexity and long-term capital needs, which management calls high barriers, but entrants with capital could overcome them, which would hit the yield on the 36.46% of capital held under other agreements first. Substitutes include traditional land banking arrangements and other providers, which the 2025 annual report says Lennar may choose instead, and the move of builders to an asset-light model supports demand. Buyer power is the central force: Lennar can match any lower rate offered to others, can pause at its own election under the 2024 terms, and supplies 69% of revenue, which limits pricing and weighs on recovery of income in a downturn. Supplier power is limited: development is performed by the counterparties, who bear cost overruns under the construction agreements, so input-cost inflation falls mainly on them, the manager's fee is set by contract, and land sellers are not described as a constraint in the documents.

REIT status is a regulatory condition of the credit agreement and drives the distribution policy, so loss of status would be an event of concern; none is reported.

### Financial profile and credit quality

Scale is meaningful, with reported EBITDA of about USD 675 million over twelve months, but the operating history is under two years and one counterparty group dominates, so market position is average rather than proven. Business diversification is weak by counterparty although improving, and about 53% of property assets sit in California, Florida and Texas, while 22 counterparties and 76 pools give some spread. The cost structure supports margin stability, but it is untested on income and cost flexibility is limited because the fee follows assets. Free cash flow after distributions is negative and is funded by borrowing, so its stability is weak. The ability to refinance looks adequate on evidence of senior note issues in August 2025, September 2025 and October 2026 at coupons of 6.25% to 6.75% and long maturities, but no rating is supplied. ### Outlook, tailwinds and headwinds

Over the next 12 to 24 months the income run rate of about USD 221 million a quarter is about USD 10 million above third-quarter revenue and management raised its year-end AFFO outlook, which supports cover as the Dream Finders and rental assets begin to earn. Against that, the full coupons of the 2029 and 2031 notes add USD 66.25 million a year of interest, the dividend of about USD 131 million was paid on October 15, 2026, and leverage depends on takedown proceeds repaying the revolving facility. Over three to five years the portfolio's weighted average remaining life of 3.3 years and weighted average maturity of 53 months fall before the 2030 maturities of 70.51% of September 30, 2026 principal, so refinancing is likely to be met from the recycling of capital if new funding continues; a stall in takedowns would turn that into a market refinancing. Sector tailwinds are builders' shift to an asset-light model and consolidation that creates funding demand; headwinds are a challenging housing market and affordability. Company-specific tailwinds are the yield spread and diversification; headwinds are leverage, concentration and distributions.

### Downside scenario

The initiating condition is a housing downturn, although none is needed: the 2024 annual report lets Lennar designate two pause periods of up to six months each on a property if a local home price index falls 10% or more or in a public health emergency, and also at its own election under its Founder's Rights, and similar provisions in other counterparty agreements could delay takedowns and cut cash inflows. In the first operational break Lennar, which paid USD 144.9 million in the third quarter, pauses takedown and construction deadlines and pays half the applicable monthly option rate: in this illustration that removes USD 72.45 million of option income a quarter, 34.34% of third-quarter revenue and 40.15% of third-quarter EBITDA, while takedown proceeds that repay the revolving facility are delayed.

The financial transmission is fast because costs are tied to assets and debt: the management fee follows assets, senior note coupons are fixed, and distributions continue at about 100% of net income. Over a six-month pause last-twelve-months EBITDA falls by USD 144.9 million to about USD 530 million, total debt to EBITDA on the September 30, 2026 balance sheet rises from 4.95x to 6.30x, net debt to EBITDA pro forma for the Dream Finders closing rises from 6.18x to 7.87x, and cover of senior note coupons falls from 3.50x to about 2.75x before floating-rate interest. If both permitted periods were used, the reduction would be USD 289.8 million, leverage 8.67x and coupon cover 2.00x, and the agreements allow two further periods by mutual agreement, up to two years in total, which would need Millrose's consent. These are sensitivities on contract terms and reported figures, not forecasts, and the effect on revenue under lease accounting is not established.

On liquidity, cash plus undrawn committed facilities of about USD 0.8 billion at September 30, 2026 faces a dividend of about USD 131 million a quarter, funding commitments to builders under contracts for USD 6.7 billion of future development, and a revolving facility whose borrowing base and covenants are not printed; the borrowing base would be the first constraint if collateral values fell. Maturities do not force a refinancing before 2028, so a pause tests covenants and market access before it tests repayment. Management levers are to leave the USD 250 million repurchase unused, to hold the dividend near the minimum REIT distribution, to slow discretionary new funding, to repay revolving borrowings from takedowns by other counterparties and to sell land. Resilience looks sufficient to avoid liquidity distress inside twelve months on contractual maturities, and insufficient to protect the 33% maximum target, because debt to capitalization is already 36.33%. The observable triggers are a pause notice, a fall in the monthly option payments received, takedown proceeds below the third-quarter level, a revolving draw that funds a distribution and any covenant disclosure.

### Overall credit view

Overall, Millrose presents an adequate business profile supported by contractual option income, pooled protection, an 85.18% EBITDA margin and a long, mostly unsecured maturity profile, but credit quality is constrained by debt of 36.33% of capitalization above the 33% maximum target, concentration in Lennar and a distribution policy that leaves growth funded by borrowing. Cash-flow durability appears adequate while takedowns continue, because option fees were 99.28% of revenue and are contractual, but it is untested in a pause. Refinancing capacity is likely to depend on continued takedown proceeds, on market acceptance of the 6.50% and 6.75% coupons paid on the 2029 and 2031 notes and on borrowing bases that are not printed. The primary downside path is a Lennar pause or builder slowdown that delays takedowns while distributions and committed funding continue, which would lift leverage to about 6.30x total debt to EBITDA on the September 30, 2026 balance sheet. Further analysis would require covenant levels, borrowing bases, ratings, the preferred instrument's ranking and covenants and borrowings after September 30, 2026. Fundamental credit completed. Top risk: a Lennar pause or takedown slowdown while leverage is above the stated target.
