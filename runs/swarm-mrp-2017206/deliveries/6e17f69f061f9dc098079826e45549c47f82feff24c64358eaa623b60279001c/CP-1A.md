## Findings

### Bottom line

Millrose's cash flows are contractual and well pooled, but concentration in Lennar and governance rights held outside creditor control limit the protection, so creditors should treat counterparty and governance risk as the main drivers of credit quality.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 40, 47

Lennar supplied 69% of third-quarter revenue and 63.54% of invested capital; its payment obligations are capped at USD 50 million per pool for spin-off assets and USD 25 million per pool for later assets, and a Lennar Corporation guarantee backs them. One family holds 98.7% of Class B stock and about 43% of the votes, Lennar keeps consent rights over the manager and over debt above one times equity, and a securities class action was filed on October 9, 2026.
Basis: calculation C1; 10-K_2025-12-31_ck0002017206-20251231.htm.jsonl, pages 32–35, 71; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 40, 75

### Key findings

Revenue durability is good but rests on one counterparty group. Lennar supplied 69% of third-quarter revenue and 71% of nine-month revenue, down from 84% in FY2025, because other counterparties now hold 36.46% of invested capital at a weighted yield of 10.8% against 8.5% on the Lennar program. Option fees are paid monthly on invested capital, so the cash that services the notes depends on option payments and takedowns from Lennar's divisions, and the caps of USD 50 million and USD 25 million per pool bound what Lennar owes if it walks from a pool whose average invested capital is USD 121.65 million. Protection after a Lennar default would therefore come mainly from resale of the land and the Lennar Corporation guarantee, not from the payment caps.
Basis: calculation C1; calculation C8; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 40, 45–47; 10-K_2025-12-31_ck0002017206-20251231.htm.jsonl, pages 32–34, 175

Contractual protection is strong in design and untested in practice. 93% of invested capital is pooled so that one counterparty's option termination can put the rest of its pool at risk, deposits are non-refundable, counterparties bear cost overruns and development obligations, and the filings report zero option terminations since inception. That record has been earned in a period without a recorded counterparty delinquency or default, so pooling, forfeiture and resale have not been exercised against a stressed builder, and the evidence supports the design rather than the outcome.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 25, 47; 10-K_2025-12-31_ck0002017206-20251231.htm.jsonl, pages 23–24, 26; EX-99.1_2026-08-04_ck0002017206-ex99_1.htm.jsonl, page 1

Growth is moving into credit-like exposures that sit outside the pool protections. The Dream Finders support totals about USD 1.1 billion, of which a preferred instrument paying a 12% dividend is USD 487.5 million at purchase cost and the rest is homesites under option agreements; development loans, reduced by a USD 284.2 million payoff, and single-family rental homes bought from Lennar for about USD 333 million add loan and operating exposure, with more homes agreed for USD 150.3 million. A preferred instrument is equity-like and ranks behind the issuer's debt, and its covenants and ranking are not described, so the preferred capital depends on one builder's post-merger performance rather than on pooled land.
Basis: calculation C9; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 26, 41, 43; EX-99.1_2026-10-26_ck0002017206-ex99_1.htm.jsonl, page 3

Governance gives creditors little direct control and leaves decisions with a manager paid on assets. Millrose has no employees; the external manager earns 1.25% of Tangible Assets a year, which was USD 30.3 million in the third quarter, or 14.34% of revenue, so its pay rises with balance-sheet growth whether the growth is funded by debt or equity. Extraordinary litigation costs fall outside the fee and are borne by Millrose, opportunities are shared with Kennedy Lewis vehicles by rotation, and the July 2026 multifamily facility was set up by the manager's parent. Replacing it without cause during the initial term would cost 2.5 times the average annual fee, about USD 219.38 million on the FY2025 fee. Lenders hold one lever: the credit agreement defaults if the manager ceases to act and no replacement acceptable to lenders is appointed within 90 days, and the manager can itself leave on 60 days' notice after an uncured default.
Basis: calculation C4; calculation C12; 10-K_2025-12-31_ck0002017206-20251231.htm.jsonl, pages 27–28, 30, 35, 70; 10-K_2024-12-31_d931976d10k.htm.jsonl, page 28; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 31, 43, 48

Control rights held by Lennar and the Miller Family constrain the board in ways that can protect or hinder creditors. Class B stock held by the Miller Family carries about 43% of the votes and has a separate class vote on mergers and Class B issuance, and Lennar's rights include a ceiling of one times debt to equity (0.57x at September 30, 2026), consent over secured financing that mixes Lennar and other assets, and consent over a replacement manager until the sunset threshold, which Lennar's contributed assets falling below 10% of total assets for six consecutive months would trigger. The ceiling protects unsecured creditors from leverage above one times equity, but the August 27, 2026 amendment to these rights is not described, so whether each right still binds is not established.
Basis: calculation C5; 10-K_2025-12-31_ck0002017206-20251231.htm.jsonl, pages 34–35, 71, 84–85; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 65–66, 76

Capital allocation favors shareholders while leverage sits above the stated target. Cash dividends paid in the nine months to September 30, 2026 were 99.94% of net income and 97.81% of AFFO, the dividend rose in each quarter to USD 0.79 a share, and the board authorized a USD 250 million buyback on October 26, 2026 with no repurchase stated. The third-quarter release states debt to capitalization of 36% against the 33% maximum management set in the first quarter, so equity returns and buybacks draw on the same cushion that protects creditors; distributions of at least 90% of REIT taxable income are a requirement of the REIT regime and leave little retained cash.
Basis: calculation C6; EX-99.1_2026-05-06_ck0002017206-ex99_1.htm.jsonl, page 3; EX-99.1_2026-10-26_ck0002017206-ex99_1.htm.jsonl, pages 2, 4; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 36, 41, 66

A securities class action filed on October 9, 2026 adds an unquantified legal cost and a disclosure question. The complaint covers purchasers from February 7, 2025 to October 2, 2026 and claims under the Exchange Act, seeks unspecified damages, and Millrose says the claims have no merit; the management fee does not cover extraordinary litigation, so costs and any award fall on Millrose. The earlier source-readiness review did not record this event.
Basis: Source readiness (CP-0); 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 34, 48, 75

### Contrary evidence and what would change the view

The strongest evidence against concern is that the structure has performed: no property has been terminated, 93% of capital is pooled, the Lennar share of revenue fell from 84% in FY2025 to 69%, 22 counterparties now hold 76 pools, the debt-to-equity ratio of 0.57x is well inside Lennar's ceiling of one times equity, and the annual report schedules undiscounted option fees at December 31, 2025 that extend beyond 2030. Asset yield also exceeds funding cost: management states a 9.3% portfolio yield against an average coupon of 6.43% on USD 3,000 million of senior notes outstanding after October 6, 2026.

The view would improve if counterparties outside Lennar were shown to carry guarantees comparable to Lennar's, if the August 2026 amendment removed none of the consent rights that limit leverage, and if the class action were dismissed. It would worsen on any pool-level termination by Lennar or by Dream Finders, a Lennar rating action if the rating-linked option pricing still applies, use of Lennar's consent rights to block financing, a sunset event that removes the leverage ceiling, or a move of the preferred instrument or rental homes into impairment.
Basis: calculation C2; calculation C5; calculation C7; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 29, 45–47; 10-K_2025-12-31_ck0002017206-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 174

### Transaction summary and new debt issues

Millrose was created by Lennar's spin-off on February 7, 2025, which contributed about USD 5.5 billion of land assets and about USD 1.0 billion of cash that included USD 585 million of option deposit liabilities. Since then it has bought the Rausch homesites on February 10, 2025 for about USD 859 million net of deposits, funded USD 494.5 million at the June 25, 2025 closing of New Home's acquisition of Landsea, closed its first multifamily land banking acquisition with JPI on July 30, 2026, and supported Dream Finders' acquisition of Beazer Homes with funding of about USD 1.068 billion across September 14 and October 20, 2026. The FY2024 annual report gave about USD 876 million for the Rausch purchase, a difference logged in the conflict register.
Basis: 10-K_2025-12-31_ck0002017206-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 18; 10-K_2024-12-31_d931976d10k.htm.jsonl, page 216; 10-Q_2026-09-30_ck0002017206-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 41, 43

New debt came in four steps. The company issued USD 1.25 billion of senior notes due 2030 on August 7, 2025 and USD 750 million due 2032 on September 11, 2025, replaced its secured revolving facility with an unsecured credit agreement on March 25, 2026, signed a limited-recourse rental facility of up to USD 235 million on September 30, 2026, and closed USD 1.0 billion of senior notes due 2029 and 2031 on October 6, 2026.
