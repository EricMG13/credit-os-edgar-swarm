## Findings

Option Care Health's stand-alone credit profile is adequate, with moderate reported leverage, strong liquidity and thin margins, but the pending take-private makes that profile historical because the post-closing capital structure is not established.

### Company description

Option Care Health is a single-segment provider of home and alternate-site infusion services. It dispenses drugs and gives nursing support and care coordination to patients at home or in ambulatory infusion suites, and is paid mostly by managed care organizations, insurers, employers, Medicare and Medicaid. Net revenue was USD 5,649.519 million in FY2025 and the company reports 87 full service pharmacies and 188 suites. Cash flow is visible because chronic therapies repeat and the company positions home infusion as a lower-cost alternative to the hospital, but visibility is moderate: referral sources are not contractually obliged to refer, payer contracts can be re-bid, and acute therapies are episodic. These traits support debt service while payer terms hold, and they weaken refinancing confidence if margin keeps falling.

### Ownership and group structure

Until the merger agreement of 2026-10-05 the company was publicly traded with no controlling holder named in the filings supplied and no dividend ever paid. Option Care Health, Inc. is the borrower and issuer, the term loan is secured by first-priority liens on substantially all assets of the company and its subsidiaries, and the notes are senior unsecured; guarantors are named in an indenture that is not supplied. Business & governance assessed sponsor and governance risk as medium on the stand-alone record: no dividends, regular reporting and interim limits in the merger agreement, against buybacks above free cash flow in 2025. The new owners, affiliates of Clayton, Dubilier & Rice and McKesson, have no behavior history in the sources, so their financial policy, dividend appetite and treatment of lenders are not established and are not inferred from identity.

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Revenue is recurring in chronic therapies and episodic in acute care, concentrated in payer contract terms, partly regulated and exposed to published pricing benchmarks. Growth was 13.03% in 2025, with acute therapies growing at a mid-teens rate helped by shifts in the competitive landscape and chronic therapies at a low double-digit rate; in the first nine months of 2026 growth was 2.45%, with acute at a high single-digit rate and chronic flat as the chronic inflammatory portfolio declined on patient attrition and unfavourable mix. Pricing power is limited: reimbursement is set at a discount to average wholesale price or a premium to average sales price, payers can make the company bid against competitors, and direct government programs were about 12% of revenue in 2025 and about 13% in the nine months of 2026. Revenue is not structurally declining, but its growth has slowed and the 2025 acute surge may not repeat.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Costs are mostly bought-in drugs, 70.84% of nine-month 2026 revenue, then salaries and benefits at 15.26% and other operating items at 6.96%, with SG&A at 12.1% of revenue. The filings do not split fixed from variable costs. Drug cost moves with volume and mix, so operating leverage comes from the labor and overhead base, which SG&A discipline has held to a falling share of revenue; but pass-through is limited because reimbursement follows benchmarks, and labor shortages have raised costs in some markets. Margins can be defended against modest volume pressure, but not against a payer rate cut, adverse mix or supplier price rise: gross margin has already fallen 3.55 percentage points from 2023 to 2025.

### Competitive forces

Rivalry is intense and fragmented: named competitors include units of UnitedHealth Group and CVS Health and other national and regional infusion companies, some owned by payers or distributors, which lets them price and source drugs more cheaply in some therapies; this pressures price and referral share. Entry barriers are moderate because pharmacy licensing, sterile compounding standards, accreditation and payer network access take time; the credit effect is that new entrants arrive slowly but integrated payers already sit in the market. Substitutes are hospital and physician-office infusion, which the company counters with ambulatory suites; they matter only if payers redirect volume. Buyer power is high: managed care consolidation and re-bidding limit pricing and can remove access to patients. Supplier power is also high: a few vendors supply most purchases, manufacturers can cancel contracts on minimal notice and some drugs have limited distribution. Together these forces explain a thin margin and mean the first strain on credit would appear in gross margin, not in liquidity.

### External factors

Regulatory and payer policy are material to credit and are run here; other categories are covered in less detail. Regulatory: Medicare payments face reductions of up to 2% a year through 2027, state Medicaid budgets are tight, and the 12% of revenue paid by direct government programs is exposed to fee schedules, audits and recoupment, while changes to the pricing benchmarks that set commercial reimbursement could reduce revenue per therapy across payers. Economic: the term loan is floating and the USD 300.0 million rate cap expires on 2026-11-30, so each 100 bp adds about USD 6.71 million of annual interest, small against EBITDA but real against free cash flow; labor shortages raise wage costs. Technological: the 2024 cyber incident at a third-party technology provider showed reliance on outside billing systems, and the company names AI adoption as a competitive requirement. Social factors are not a primary credit driver in the documents.

### Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats

### Financial profile and credit quality

### Downside scenario

The initiating condition is a payer re-bid or benchmark change that lowers revenue per therapy, compounded by the continued chronic inflammatory decline and biosimilar mix shift already flagged by management. The first operational break is gross margin: with labor and overhead assumed largely fixed in the short run (the filings do not split them), a one percentage point fall in EBITDA margin removes USD 57.52 million, 13.65% of last-twelve-months EBITDA. Financially, reported net leverage would rise from 2.04x to 2.36x, total leverage from 2.75x to 3.18x and cover would fall from 6.74x to 5.82x, with free cash flow lower and the 2026 inventory release not repeating. Liquidity remains ample: USD 299.1 million of cash and USD 846.0 million of revolver, with no large maturity until the 2029 notes, which equal 1.78 years of last-twelve-months free cash flow. Management levers are SG&A, inventory management and, until closing, the merger bar on buybacks and dividends; capital spending is already small. On the stand-alone structure resilience appears sufficient to avoid liquidity distress; the scenario changes sharply if buyout debt is added, because interest would take more of the lower EBITDA. The observable triggers are gross margin, payer re-bids, inventory-adjusted operating cash flow and the closing capital structure.

### Overall credit view

Overall, Option Care Health presents an adequate business profile supported by payer breadth, scale and low capital intensity, but credit quality is constrained by thin and falling margin, supplier concentration and an unsized buyout. Cash-flow durability appears adequate: free cash flow was positive every year but the 2026 gain came from inventory. Refinancing capacity is likely to depend on whether the existing facilities stay in place at closing and on how much debt the new owners raise. The primary downside path is payer pricing and therapy mix eroding margin while buyout debt reduces the time to absorb it. Further analysis would require the merger financing papers, the credit agreement and indenture, covenant levels, ratings and peer data, and therapy-level economics. Top risk: margin erosion combined with any debt raised for the change of control.
