## Findings

### Bottom line

Tenet's fundamental credit profile is adequate for a high-yield hospital operator and improving on earnings, but cash returns and Medicaid policy exposure keep deleveraging dependent on earnings growth, so creditors should expect stable rather than strengthening credit quality. Net debt to issuer-adjusted EBITDA fell to 2.11x at 2026-09-30 from 2.25x at 2025-12-31 because earnings rose while net debt grew by USD 281 million, and liquidity of USD 4,574 million is 2.16x the USD 2,115 million of debt due through 2028.

The strongest support is liquidity ahead of the 2028 maturities and an ambulatory franchise that earns a 38.3% margin. The weakest dimension is free cash flow after returns: repurchases and distributions to partners took 95.09% of free cash flow excluding the CommonSpirit offset, and the primary downside path runs from lower Medicaid payments and fewer exchange-covered admissions in 2027 to hospital earnings.

The strongest evidence for a better view is that last-twelve-month Adjusted EBITDA is above its 2024 level by 25.26%, net leverage is 2.11x with USD 4,574 million of liquidity, and the returns that absorb 95.09% of free cash flow are largely discretionary: the repurchase program can be suspended, which would release up to USD 1,813 million a year at the last-twelve-month pace, and the CommonSpirit note adds about USD 453 million a year from January 2027, equal to 24.99% of those repurchases. ### Business profile and credit linkage

Tenet is a scaled, diversified operator whose cash flows are anchored in hospitals but are increasingly earned in outpatient surgery. It operated 51 hospitals in eight states at 2026-09-30, with about 74% of its hospital outpatient centers in Arizona and Texas at 2025-12-31, and USPI runs a network of surgery centers and surgical hospitals in 37 states; last-twelve-month net operating revenues were USD 22,154 million. Hospitals produced 75.47% of nine-month revenue and 59.09% of Adjusted EBITDA, so a payer or regulatory shock in a few states reaches a large share of cash flow at once, while the ambulatory network spreads procedure volume across many facilities and physician partners.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_thc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 26; 10-K_2025-12-31_thc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 6; 10-K_2025-12-31_thc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 7; Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C46

Revenue is transactional and regulated, not contracted: managed care supplies 68.51% of hospital patient service revenue, Medicare 15.19% and Medicaid 11.90%, so cash-flow visibility depends on payer contract renewals and government rate-setting, not on long-dated customer contracts. That supports a steady cash profile in normal conditions, because acute care volumes grew in 2026, but it gives creditors little contractual protection when payer mix or rates change.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_thc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 17; EX-99.1_2026-10-29_thc-20260930ex991earningsr.htm.jsonl, page 4; Business & governance (CP-1A)

The portfolio has been reshaped by disposals and financing events that matter for credit. In 2024 Tenet sold fourteen hospitals and six surgery centers, with USD 4,981 million of proceeds from sales of facilities and other assets, in the year it redeemed USD 2,100 million of secured notes and repurchased USD 672 million of shares; in 2025 it acquired controlling interests in 27 surgery centers and one surgical hospital; and in 2026 it ended the CommonSpirit services contract and refinanced the 2027 secured notes into 2034 unsecured notes. These steps lowered the near-term maturity wall and moved the business toward outpatient care, which the issuer says has generated significantly higher margins than inpatient services, at the cost of a revenue cycle contract that ends and a stack in which 62.21% of principal is still secured.

Ownership is public and no controlling holder or sponsor is named, which removes the usual dividend recapitalization and fee extraction channels, but control is not confirmed because board composition and beneficial ownership sit in a proxy statement that was not supplied. The structure matters more for ranking than for ownership: the secured notes are guaranteed by wholly owned domestic hospital subsidiaries and secured by a pledge of their capital stock, the unsecured notes carry no described subsidiary guarantee and are effectively subordinated to the secured notes and to subsidiary obligations, and 62.21% of principal is secured notes, so unsecured creditors rely on residual value after secured claims and minority partners.

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Hospital growth is volume-led, with pricing held back by payer mix in the third quarter. Same-hospital admissions rose 2.8% in the third quarter and 1.8% in the nine months, adjusted admissions rose 3.2% and 2.1%, but net patient service revenue per adjusted admission rose only 0.2% in the third quarter because strength in commercial employer revenue was offset by unfavorable payer mix from fewer exchange admissions. Pricing power is therefore constrained where the mix shifts toward Medicaid and uninsured patients, and revenue stays predictable only while coverage holds.
Basis: EX-99.1_2026-10-29_thc-20260930ex991earningsr.htm.jsonl, page 4; 10-Q_2026-09-30_thc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 28; 10-Q_2026-09-30_thc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 35

Ambulatory growth is price and acuity-led. Same-facility system-wide revenue rose 5.1% in the nine months with revenue per case up 6.0% and surgical cases down 0.8%, and the third-quarter case decline was 1.5%, so growth depends on the pricing and mix of procedures, not on rising volumes, which leaves it exposed to payer pushback on rates.
Basis: EX-99.1_2026-10-29_thc-20260930ex991earningsr.htm.jsonl, page 3

Concentration and contract terms shape renewal risk. The top ten managed care payers produced 69% of managed care patient revenue in 2025, 67% of net hospital accounts receivable was due from managed care payers at 2025-12-31, and the issuer says competitors may negotiate exclusivity or narrow networks, so a lost or repriced contract at one large payer would move hospital revenue in affected regions. Revenue is regulated, payer-concentrated and not structurally declining, with volume stable and price the swing factor.
Basis: 10-K_2025-12-31_thc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 14; 10-K_2025-12-31_thc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 30

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Labor is the dominant cost and the main source of operating leverage. Salaries, wages and benefits were 39.6% of nine-month net operating revenues, supplies and other operating expenses make up most of the rest, and the 1.5 percentage point fall in the labor share is worth about USD 249.41 million at nine-month revenue, so each point of labor share moves nine-month Adjusted EBITDA by 4.35%. Margins can be defended under moderate volume pressure through staffing and supply chain management, but a payer-mix shock lowers revenue per admission while staffing is largely fixed, which is how the margin gain would reverse.

Cost inflation has not gone away. The issuer reports shortages of critical-care nurses and advanced practice clinicians, state minimum wage increases in California through 2028, more expensive temporary and contract labor when needed, high medical supply price inflation in 2025 and possible tariff effects, and malpractice expense that rose 21.81% in the nine months. Pass-through is weak because rates are set by payers, so these costs reach margins unless volumes or commercial rates offset them.

### Competitive forces

Rivalry is local and moderate. Hospitals compete on quality, location, scope and physician relationships, physicians often admit to several facilities and can move patients, and some competitors are tax-supported or not-for-profit with access to tax-exempt financing and endowments, so share and pricing in a region depend on physician loyalty and payer contracts, which limits margin expansion beyond cost control.
Basis: 10-K_2025-12-31_thc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 13

New entrants and substitutes act through outpatient care. Freestanding specialty hospitals, surgery centers, emergency departments, imaging centers and urgent care centers have grown significantly in Tenet's markets, some are physician-owned, and system-affiliated hospitals, insurers and private equity buyers are expected to add competition, so inpatient volumes and higher-margin outpatient services both face competition, even though Tenet's own USPI network benefits from the shift of procedures out of hospitals.
Basis: 10-K_2025-12-31_thc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 13; 10-K_2025-12-31_thc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 60

Buyer power is high. Managed care payers use discounts for network access, the top ten payers produced 69% of managed care revenue, and government programs set rates that Tenet cannot negotiate, so pricing gains depend on commercial renewals and the payer mix.

Supplier power sits mainly in labor and physicians. Nurses, therapists and advanced practice clinicians are scarce, wage and contract-labor costs rose with inflation, and physicians can end their association with a facility at any time, so labor supply is the force most able to compress margins.

### Policy, macro and technology factors

Political and regulatory factors are a primary credit driver. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act adds Medicaid work requirements, caps on state-directed payments and limits on provider taxes from 2027, Medicaid disproportionate share reductions are scheduled from 2027-10-01, and enhanced premium credits for marketplace plans expired at the end of 2025; four of the eight states in which Tenet operates hospitals have expanded Medicaid, and over half of licensed beds are in four states that have not. The mechanism is fewer insured patients and lower or delayed supplemental payments, which reduces hospital revenue per admission and, because supplemental payments are part of the revolver borrowing base, could also reduce borrowing availability.

### Overall credit view

Overall, Tenet presents an adequate business profile supported by scale, an ambulatory earnings base and committed liquidity, but credit quality is constrained by hospital payer-policy exposure and by returns that absorb 95.09% of free cash flow. Cash-flow durability appears adequate because interest cover is 6.03x and free cash flow converts about 55% of Adjusted EBITDA, but it depends on earnings holding near the current level. Refinancing capacity is likely to depend on stable earnings through the 2028 maturities of USD 1,931 million and on market conditions, because debt has been refinanced and not repaid. The primary downside path is a Medicaid and exchange-driven payer-mix shift in 2027 that lowers hospital earnings while shareholder returns continue. Compared with other leveraged hospital issuers the profile cannot be placed, because no peer data were supplied. Further analysis would require covenant ratio levels and executed documents, the size of Medicaid supplemental payments, the earnings of the ended services contract and the proxy statement. The top risk is payer-policy pressure on hospital earnings.
