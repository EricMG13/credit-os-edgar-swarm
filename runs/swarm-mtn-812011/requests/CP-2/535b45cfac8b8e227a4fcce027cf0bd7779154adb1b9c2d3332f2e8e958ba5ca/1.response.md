## Findings

### Bottom line

Vail Resorts is an adequate but weakening credit: a weather-driven 12.72% fall in Total Reported EBITDA lifted total leverage to 4.23x from 3.70x with debt flat, and payouts above free cash flow leave less room for a second weak season.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 101, 112, 132, 135; Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation C04; calculation C01; calculation C10

Creditors have time but not much cushion: only 7.08% of principal falls due before fiscal 2030, the issuer holds USD 231.3 million of cash and USD 513.3 million of undrawn revolver commitments, and debt due within a year is 11.27% of that liquidity, yet cash interest cover fell to 4.04x from 5.52x and the covenant levels are not printed, so headroom cannot be measured. The effect of the dividend payable on 2026-10-27 on cash and leverage is not established.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 102, 114, 120, 132, 167; Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation C13; calculation C11; calculation C12; calculation C03

The effect of the dividend payable on 2026-10-27 on net leverage is not established.

### Contrary evidence and what would change the view

The strongest evidence against a cautious view is that fiscal 2026 was an extreme weather year and the issuer still held the fall in EBITDA to 12.72% on a 13.4% fall in skier visits, because effective ticket price rose 11.5%, passes were about 70% of lift revenue and costs flexed with USD 45 million of efficiency savings. The dividend alone was 1.28x free cash flow, so a cut would restore coverage; almost all debt matures in fiscal 2030 or later; the issuer reports compliance with every financial covenant and expects it for the next twelve months; and total leverage would return to 3.69x if EBITDA recovered to the fiscal 2025 level with debt unchanged.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 99, 101, 103, 117, 120, 167; Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C04; calculation C10; calculation C14

The view would improve if pass sales for 2026/2027 close near the prior year, if the dividend is reset inside free cash flow and if the executed debt agreements confirm covenant room. It would worsen if a second weak season repeats the fiscal 2026 change, which on the arithmetic above takes leverage to 4.87x and leaves dividends above free cash flow by an amount equal to 22.51% of liquidity, if revolver borrowings at the next year end exceed USD 180.0 million, or if lenders require an amendment before the 2030 maturities enter the one-year window in 2029.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 101, 102, 156; calculation C15; calculation C23

Vail Resorts operates 42 resorts, four of the ten most visited in the United States, with 17.9% of North American skier visits, and fiscal 2026 showed the benefit when eastern resorts held visitation better while the Rockies and Tahoe lost it, so one region failing does not stop the whole business from producing cash.
  Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 8, 13, 51, 104; Business & governance (CP-1A)

- Strength: the pass model steadies revenue and brings cash in before the season. Pass products were about 70% of lift revenue, effective ticket price rose 11.5% while visits fell 13.4%, and deferred revenue of USD 566.5 million was collected before winter, so a weak season hits earnings through fixed costs more than through lost prepaid revenue.
  Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 99, 103, 150; Business & governance (CP-1A)

### Business, ownership and revenue durability

Vail Resorts operates 42 destination mountain resorts and regional ski areas, and the Mountain segment produced 96.85% of Total Reported EBITDA in fiscal 2026 while Lodging added only a small share. Revenue comes mainly from lift tickets, which are about 58% of Mountain revenue, and passes are about 70% of lift revenue, so the business model gives visibility through advance sales but ties debt service to snowfall and to pass renewals.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 97, 99, 183; Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C16

Ownership is dispersed with no controlling holder, the parent is a holding company whose bank borrower is a wholly owned subsidiary, and distribution limits at Whistler and Peak Resorts and noncontrolling interests below the parent reduce the cash that reaches creditors of the parent. Business & governance rates sponsor and governance risk Medium because no creditor-adverse action is documented, but the proxy statement that would show board independence and holders is not supplied.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 7, 82, 133, 158; Business & governance (CP-1A)

Revenue is discretionary and weather-exposed rather than contractual: paid lift revenue fell 17.5% and ski school, dining and retail revenue fell between 6.5% and 10.3%, while pass revenue rose USD 38.8 million and Destination guests rose to 58% of destination-resort visits. The consequence for creditors is that a poor season hurts ancillary revenue first, and the Epic Coverage refund terms would reduce pass revenue if a season closes early.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 23, 53, 98, 103, 104; Business & governance (CP-1A)

### Cost structure, margin resilience and cash conversion

Costs have a significant fixed component, so margins fall faster than revenue: EBITDA margin was 26.53% against 29.10% and 74.65% of each lost revenue dollar reached EBITDA. Margins can be defended partly, because labour hours flexed with visits and USD 45 million of efficiency savings were earned, but a second poor season would find fewer easy savings, since the two-year programme that delivered them has been executed and the next phase is described but not quantified.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 38, 99, 101, 104, 183; Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C04; calculation C16

Cash conversion is the weakest part of the profile: free cash flow equalled 32.93% of EBITDA against 37.05% a year earlier and 45.71% two years earlier, because cash interest and capital expenditure now absorb 55.50% of EBITDA. Capital expenditure barely moved, at USD 231.625 million against USD 235.191 million, and the earnings left after cash interest, capital expenditure and cash taxes were below the dividend by USD 51.960 million before working capital, so the payout is the variable that decides whether creditors see deleveraging.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 112, 135; Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation C06; calculation C07; calculation C08

### Market forces and external factors

Competition comes from multi-resort passes sold by rivals and from other destinations, and the weaker 2026/2027 pass sales show that price and value are contested; entry into destination skiing is difficult, which the issuer says has produced no new destination resort of scale in North America for over 45 years, so the competitive pressure falls on price and mix rather than on new capacity. Labour and employee housing are the main supplier pressures, which is why the cost of staffing is a margin risk. The effect for creditors is a ceiling on pricing power and a floor on costs.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 13, 19, 21, 101; Business & governance (CP-1A)

Regulation and macro conditions matter through four channels: permits and concessions that set the right to operate and the fee paid, discretionary consumer spending that follows employment and inflation, interest rates on the variable-rate share of debt after a 25 bp increase following the September 2026 meeting, and currency effects on the Canadian, Australian and Swiss earnings. Of these, weather and rates move credit metrics directly, while technology and social trends are not a primary credit driver on the filings supplied.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 40, 41, 48, 50, 127; Business & governance (CP-1A)

### Financial profile and credit quality

Scale and market position are strong, supported by 42 resorts and 17.9% of North American visits. Competitive advantage and business diversification are average, because the pass ecosystem lifts yield but 96.85% of EBITDA comes from one weather-exposed segment. Cost and capex flexibility and margin stability are average, with real levers but a high decremental margin. Free cash flow stability is weak, with conversion down for two years and payouts above it. Ability to refinance is average, with notes issued at par in 2024 and 2025 and the bank facilities extended in February 2026 but 54.62% of principal falling due in fiscal 2030. Liquidity is average, adequate for twelve months but at the seasonal low and 35.93% lower than a year earlier, and financial policy and governance are average and trending weaker. The effect of the dividend payable on 2026-10-27 on this liquidity is not established.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 13, 112, 135, 158, 160, 161; Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C06; calculation C13; calculation C11

The trend is a gradual weakening: total leverage rose from 3.38x to 3.70x to 4.23x, cash interest cover fell from 5.64x to 5.52x to 4.04x and free cash flow fell for two years. The main supports are the pass model, the flexible dividend and the distant maturities; the main constraints are the concentration of earnings, the payout and the absence of verifiable covenant room; and the missing data are the executed debt agreements, the proxy statement and any rating or peer evidence.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 112, 135; Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation C01; calculation C03

### Outlook, tailwinds and headwinds

Over the next twelve to twenty-four months the weaker 2026/2027 pass sales and lower prepaid cash are the first headwind, partly offset by a full year of efficiency savings, effective ticket price that rose 11.5%, a capital plan with discretionary projects and Swiss growth capital of USD 12.0 million; variable-rate debt of about USD 1.5 billion means each 100 bp costs USD 14.7 million a year, and the 25 bp rise after the September 2026 meeting adds to that cost. Over three to five years the cluster of maturities in fiscal 2030, permit expiries from 2029, the step-up in the Grand Teton fee from 2027 and climate variability are the headwinds, while pricing, pass mix and digital engagement are the tailwinds the issuer cites.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 48, 50, 101, 103, 115, 127, 167; Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C20; calculation C23

### Downside scenario

A second season with weak western snowfall is the realistic downside, because fiscal 2026 showed how it works. Poor early-season conditions cut visits and paid lift revenue, ancillary revenue falls with them, pass renewals for the following season weaken as they already have for 2026/2027, and the fixed cost base passes about three quarters of each lost revenue dollar to EBITDA. Repeating the fiscal 2026 change in EBITDA with everything else unchanged takes EBITDA 13.08% lower, total leverage to 4.87x and cash interest cover to 3.51x, and leaves dividends above free cash flow by an amount equal to 22.51% of liquidity, which the revolver and cash can fund for a year but which would take drawn revolver borrowings above their level at the 2026 year end.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 101, 112, 135, 183; Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C15; calculation C11

Management levers are real: a dividend cut of 52.85% would close the shortfall, repurchases can stay suspended, capital spending has discretionary projects and cash taxes would fall with earnings. Resilience therefore appears sufficient to avoid a liquidity event within twelve months, but not to avoid a further rise in leverage unless the dividend is reset, and the covenant level that would bound the revolver is not printed, so the point at which lenders would require action is not established.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 94, 115, 117, 119, 120; calculation C15; calculation C10

### Overall credit view

Overall, Vail Resorts presents an adequate business profile supported by an owned network of 42 resorts, a pass model that brings cash in before the season and costs and capital spending that can be flexed, but credit quality is constrained by earnings concentrated in one weather-exposed segment, total leverage of 4.23x and cash interest cover of 4.04x, and payouts above free cash flow. Cash-flow durability appears adequate for one weak season and weak for two, because free cash flow conversion has fallen to 32.93% of EBITDA. Refinancing capacity is likely to depend on leverage when the fiscal 2030 maturities approach, which enter the one-year window in 2029. The primary downside path is a second weak season with weak pass sales, and further analysis would require the executed debt agreements, the proxy statement and the pass sales outcome for 2026/2027. Top risk: a repeat of the fiscal 2026 earnings fall while dividends stay above free cash flow.
Basis: 10-K_2026-07-31_mtn-20260731.htm.jsonl, pages 112, 135, 167; Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C01; calculation C03; calculation C06; calculation C23
