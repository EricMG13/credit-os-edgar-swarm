## Findings

### Bottom line

Tidewater's fundamental credit profile is solid but weakening: leverage is low and liquidity strong, yet margins are falling in a short-contract, drydock-heavy business, so creditors are exposed mainly to earnings erosion ahead of the 2030 refinancing and to the newly acquired Brazilian fleet.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 38–39; calculation D01; calculation D02

Net debt was 0.92x last-twelve-months EBITDA at 2026-09-30 and cash interest cover 8.03x, with USD 640,844 thousand of cash and undrawn revolver, but the nine-month 2026 EBITDA margin fell to 36.89% from 40.82% and USD 682,474 thousand of principal falls due in 2030. The parent replaced the former owners as guarantor of the Brazilian construction loans on 2026-10-01 and the replacement bank guarantees ended on 2026-10-02, and any effect on net debt or revolver availability is not established.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 19; calculation D01; calculation D02; calculation D06

Covenant headroom, ratings and legal capacity are not established because no executed debt document or rating was supplied.
Basis: Source readiness (CP-0)

Pro forma net debt including the acquisition for 2025-12-31 is not established.
  Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 13; calculation D01

### Contrary evidence and what would change the view

The strongest evidence against the view that the profile is weakening is that part of the margin fall is probably transitory: the nine-month 2026 result carries USD 14.4 million of acquisition costs and about USD 9.0 million of conflict-related crew, insurance and fuel cost, third-quarter EBITDA was only 3.11% below the same quarter a year earlier, free cash flow remains well above interest and the notes trade above principal. The view would turn positive if a full quarter of the acquired fleet adds earnings and the conflict costs fade, and negative if the 8.48% nine-month EBITDA decline continues, if West Africa keeps shrinking, or if the revolver covenant definitions prove tighter than derived leverage suggests.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 8; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 33; Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation D03; calculation D07; calculation D10

### Strengths and weaknesses

Tidewater's strongest support is a low-leverage balance sheet: net debt of 0.92x derived EBITDA and cash interest cover of 8.03x leave room before debt service is stressed, which lowers near-term default risk. Liquidity of USD 640,844 thousand against debt due through 2029 of USD 141,707 thousand means all maturities through 2029 can be paid from cash, and free cash flow of USD 270,068 thousand, 52.98% of EBITDA, funds drydocking internally. A footprint of five segments in over thirty countries, with no customer at 10% of revenue in 2025, limits the effect of a single-market shock, and the first large maturity is in 2030.

West Africa produced 40.93% of FY2025 segment profit and then weakened, so one region sets much of the cash flow, and the Wilson acquisition brings integration cost, parent guarantees of USD 189,678 thousand and provisional accounting, while legal documents that would show distribution and debt capacity were not supplied.

### Company description

Tidewater time-charters offshore support vessels to oil and gas, offshore construction and windfarm customers in over thirty countries through five geographic segments, and platform supply vessels produced 72.3% of FY2025 vessel revenue against 24.2% for anchor handling towing supply vessels. The fleet is more than two hundred vessels with an average age of 14.0 years since the 22 platform supply vessels of the Wilson Companies joined on 2026-08-31, up from 13.1 years at the end of 2025. Because revenue follows utilization and day rates on contracts that are often a year or less, the cash flow that services debt is visible only a few quarters ahead, and an older fleet needs recurring drydock spending that competes with debt service.
Basis: 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 3–6; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 70; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 30; Business & governance (CP-1A)

### Ownership and group structure

Tidewater is publicly held with no sponsor or controlling holder named in the supplied documents, so there is no sponsor support to count on and no fund-life exit pressure; owner behaviour is rated medium risk because repurchases, acquisitions funded from cash and assumed debt and an unused authorization let value leave or leverage rise ahead of creditors. Debt sits at three levels: senior unsecured notes issued by the parent and guaranteed by subsidiaries, a senior secured revolver that is undrawn, and subsidiary-level secured borrowings of USD 239,990 thousand principal, held against vessels or bank guarantees and including the acquired Brazilian loans, so the USD 650.0 million of notes is unsecured alongside secured claims on specific assets, with intercreditor ranking not established.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 13–14; calculation D11

### Revenue durability

Revenue is cyclical, short-dated and linked to offshore oil and gas spending. FY2025 utilization fell to 76.1% from 79.0% while average day rates rose 6.1%, and in the nine months of 2026 utilization rose to 78.1% from 75.3% with day rates up 1.1%, so rate gains are small and volume is the swing factor. Customer concentration is moderate, with the five largest customers at 29.8% and the ten largest at 47.9% of FY2025 revenue, and a Mexican customer paid USD 54.0 million of overdue amounts in late 2025, so counterparty collection is a live risk; the credit consequence is that a utilization slump of a few points would hit revenue immediately because contracts can be ended on 90 days or less notice.
Basis: 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 8; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 40; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 74–75; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 86; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 38–39

### Margin resilience

Margins are exposed to operating leverage and cost inflation. The issuer states that a sizeable part of vessel operating and depreciation cost does not change with revenue, crew cost is 59.11% of vessel operating cost, and customers pay fuel while a vessel is on hire but the issuer pays it when idle or in drydock. The EBITDA margin fell to 36.89% in nine months from 40.82%, with Middle East war bonuses, insurance and fuel and acquisition costs as the stated drivers, so margin can be defended only if day rates keep rising faster than crew and conflict cost, and the third-quarter margin of 34.69% was below the nine-month 36.89%, so pressure had not eased by September even though third-quarter EBITDA was only 3.11% below a year earlier against 8.48% for the nine months.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 30–31; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 74; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 38–39; calculation D02; calculation D03; calculation D05

### Free cash flow conversion

Free cash flow after drydock and tax is positive and well covered, but conversion depends on cycles the issuer does not control. Last-twelve-months operating cash flow of USD 314,403 thousand less additions to properties and equipment of USD 44,335 thousand left USD 270,068 thousand, 52.98% of EBITDA, after USD 101,751 thousand of drydock cash that EBITDA adds back through amortization, and tax charges were 63.83% of pre-tax income in the nine months, so conversion would fall if drydock timing bunches or tax burden stays high. Capex is modest and discretionary beyond drydocking, which gives flexibility, while acquisition integration cost and the payments on finance leases of USD 24,903 thousand in the nine months of 2026 consumed cash.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 3–4; calculation D08; calculation D09

### Industry forces

Rivalry is broad, with many competitors of all sizes, and competes on vessel suitability, availability and price, so day rates follow the supply of vessels against offshore spending. Buyers are large oil and gas companies and national oil companies on short contracts, which gives them power at renewal, while crew labour supplies the largest cost and local rules on content and ownership protect some markets such as US Gulf trade and raise cost in others. Substitutes are limited for offshore logistics, and windfarm support offers a partial alternative market; new entrants face high vessel cost but local-content rules can also exclude Tidewater from some contracts.
Basis: 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 4; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 9; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 17; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 40

### Policy, currency and geopolitical factors

Geopolitical and currency factors are material. The Iran conflict that began on 2026-02-28 raised crew, insurance and fuel cost at the Middle East segment, African customers pay locally so cash accumulates in currencies that are hard to repatriate, the Brazilian development bank loan terms require a domestic content percentage of at least 60%, and Pillar Two and US taxes lifted the tax charge. The economic factor is the oil price: the issuer says sustained prices in the low $60s per barrel may delay some drilling projects expected to start in 2026, which would weigh on day rates and utilization.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 11; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 16; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 20; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 33; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 73

### Financial profile and credit quality

Scale and market position are strong: the group had revenue of USD 1,352,786 thousand in FY2025 and a fleet that grew with the acquisition, although the claim of the broadest operating footprint is management language. Competitive advantage is average because contracts are short and local-content rules help only in some markets. Business diversification is average: five segments and no customer at 10% of revenue in 2025, offset by 40.93% of segment profit in West Africa. Cost and capex flexibility is average, with crew cost at 59.11% of vessel operating cost and drydocking a recurring need but little discretionary capex. Margin stability is average and declining, free cash flow stability is average with USD 270,068 thousand of conversion, and the ability to refinance is average because the 2030 maturities are concentrated although the notes trade above principal. Liquidity is strong on stated figures, and financial policy and governance is average, with medium risk from repurchases and the acquisition. The trend is weaker margins and higher leverage after the acquisition, and the key missing data are covenant definitions, ratings and acquired earnings.

### Outlook, tailwinds and headwinds

Over the next 12 to 24 months the issuer's own outlook is positive on offshore investment and energy security, but it also expects conflict-related costs to continue if the conflict does; tailwinds are higher European and Mediterranean day rates, a full quarter of the acquired fleet and no maturity of size before 2030, while headwinds are crew and insurance cost, integration cost, weaker West Africa activity and any project delays from low oil prices. Over three to five years the 2030 notes become callable at a premium from 2027-07-15 and at par from 2029-07-15, which opens a refinancing window ahead of the 2030 maturity if earnings hold. The credit consequence is that headroom is large today but depends on margins stabilising before the refinancing window opens.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, pages 32–33; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 13; 10-K_2025-12-31_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 73; calculation D06

### Downside scenario

The issuer-specific downside is a prolonged Middle East conflict and project delays from low oil prices that cut utilization and day rates in the Middle East and West Africa while crew, insurance and fuel cost stay high. Revenue and EBITDA fall because contracts reprice within a year, cash interest of USD 63,484 thousand, drydock cash of USD 101,751 thousand and acquisition integration cost do not fall with them, and the free cash flow of USD 270,068 thousand is 52.98% of EBITDA, so an EBITDA decline of that share with other items unchanged would remove it; cash and revolver of USD 640,844 thousand cover debt due through 2029 of USD 141,707 thousand 4.52 times on stated figures. Management levers are vessel sales, deferral of discretionary capex, suspending repurchases and drawing the revolver, so the scenario strains the refinancing of the 2030 notes more than liquidity, and the observable triggers are margin below the Q3 2026 level, a repurchase restart and any covenant waiver.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 19; 10-Q_2026-09-30_tdw.htm.jsonl, page 33; calculation D06; calculation D08; calculation D13

### Overall credit view

Overall, Tidewater presents an adequate business profile supported by scale, geographic spread and a low-leverage balance sheet, but credit quality is constrained by falling margins, short contracts and a 2030 maturity concentration. Cash-flow durability appears adequate because free cash flow covers interest and drydocking, and refinancing capacity is likely to depend on margins recovering before the call dates of the 2030 notes and on the integration of the Brazilian fleet. The primary downside path is a conflict- and oil-price-driven fall in utilization and day rates that outruns cost flexibility before the 2030 refinancing. Further analysis would require the executed debt documents, ratings, acquired earnings and a numeric outlook. Fundamental credit completed. Top risk: margin erosion in a short-contract, drydock-heavy fleet ahead of the 2030 refinancing.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); Source readiness (CP-0); calculation D01; calculation D02; calculation D06
