### Business and ownership assessment

Fair Isaac is a US analytics software company whose profit comes mainly from Scores, a usage-based royalty on credit scores generated through consumer reporting agencies, with Software as the smaller and slower segment. This supports cash-flow visibility in activity terms but not in price terms: royalties arrive after each billing period and depend on mortgage and credit card volumes, so debt service capacity rests on activity the company does not control. Financial services supplied 92% of fiscal 2025 revenue, and revenue was USD 2,393.58 million over the last twelve months.

Ownership gives creditors no sponsor to lean on. The supplied documents identify no controlling holder, the proxy statement with board and ownership detail was not supplied, and the board approved three successively larger repurchase programmes between June 2025 and June 2026. Business & governance rates governance and financial-policy risk medium, moving toward high. The group is one registrant with unnamed consolidated subsidiaries and no stated guarantors, so the priority of the unsecured debt within the group is not established.

### Revenue durability and pricing power

Revenue is recurring in Software and usage-based in Scores, and neither is locked in for a creditor's horizon. Software recurring revenue was USD 815.8 million at 2026-06-30 and remaining performance obligations were USD 680.4 million, about half due within 14 months, which gives visibility. Scores royalties give none because distributors report volumes after the period. Pricing power is evident, since the third-quarter business-to-business increase was attributed to a higher mortgage origination scores unit price, but three consumer reporting agencies paid 63% of quarterly revenue, they sell a competing score, and the company's own risk factors say fee limits or a regulator's score-choice decision could constrain price. Price, concentration and policy risk are one risk, so revenue is predictable in Software and cyclical, concentrated and exposed to substitution in Scores.

### Margin resilience and cost flexibility

Margins are high and hard to erode through costs but can fall sharply on revenue. Scores segment costs were 10% of segment revenue for nine months, so lost price or volume falls almost entirely to operating income, while Software segment margin fell to 28% from 31% as point-in-time licence sales declined and third-party hosting costs rose. Operating expenses were 47% of revenue, with personnel and hosting the main increases, and 226 positions were removed in fiscal 2025, so cost action is available. Margins can be defended against modest volume pressure but not against a loss of Scores price.

### Industry forces

Rivalry is intense in Software and concentrated in Scores. In Software the company names fraud, origination and decisioning competitors such as Nice Actimize, Experian, Pegasystems and SAS and says many competitors are larger. In Scores the one scaled rival is VantageScore, a joint venture of the same three agencies that distribute the FICO Score. Entry is hard because the company holds no consumer credit data of its own and scores are built on agency files, but the housing regulator's validation process lets lenders use other models. Substitutes are in-house models and alternative scores, so price is the lever that most invites substitution, which is an analyst interpretation.
Basis: 10-K_2025-09-30_fico-20250930.htm.jsonl, pages 16–17; 10-K_2025-09-30_fico-20250930.htm.jsonl, page 6; 10-K_2025-09-30_fico-20250930.htm.jsonl, page 37; 10-K_2025-09-30_fico-20250930.htm.jsonl, page 38; 10-K_2025-09-30_fico-20250930.htm.jsonl, page 36

### Downside scenario

The issuer-specific downside begins with a pricing or policy event in mortgage scores, for example score choice moving volume to a competing score or a distributor winning a lower price. The first operational break is Scores revenue: segment costs are small, so the nine-month increase in Scores segment operating income of USD 363.76 million would reverse almost one for one, taking derived EBITDA from USD 1,252.44 million to USD 888.68 million. Derived gross leverage would reach 6.28x, net leverage 6.00x and, even if share-based compensation of USD 174.29 million were added back, 5.25x, against stated ceilings of 4.5x, 4.0x and 3.5x. A reversal of only half the increase gives 5.21x.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-06-30_fico-20260630.htm.jsonl, pages 32–33; 10-Q_2026-06-30_fico-20260630.htm.jsonl, page 11; Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation CALC-05

Liquidity is the second step and holds. After run-rate interest of USD 316.51 million, tax at the nine-month rate and capital spending, about USD 400.83 million of cash would remain against USD 300.00 million of term loan installments, with USD 290.00 million of undrawn revolver and repurchases as a lever. Resilience appears sufficient to avoid a liquidity crisis, but a covenant test on a definition not supplied would be the first constraint, and an amendment of the kind agreed in June 2026 could be needed before the ceiling falls to 4.0x. This is an illustration of mechanics, not a forecast or a default view.

### Overall credit view

Overall, Fair Isaac presents a strong business profile supported by a 90% Scores margin and free cash flow of USD 960.88 million, but credit quality is constrained by derived gross leverage of 4.46x built through borrowing-funded repurchases. Cash-flow durability appears adequate: conversion is high, but it depends on mortgage score price and three distributors. Refinancing capacity is likely to depend on repurchases slowing, on the covenant definition and on continued market access before the fiscal 2028 maturities of USD 1,987.50 million. The primary downside path is a pricing or policy change that reduces Scores earnings while interest and the covenant ceiling tighten. Against typical leveraged-credit issuers the profile is stronger on margin and cash conversion and weaker on leverage, but that comparison is qualitative because no peer data is supplied. Further analysis would require the executed credit agreement, the price and volume split of Scores growth and market evidence. Fundamental credit completed. Top risk: borrowing-funded repurchases against a concentrated earnings base.
