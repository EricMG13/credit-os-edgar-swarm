### Company description

CCC runs insurance claims and collision-repair workflows on a subscription software and artificial-intelligence platform, so its cash flow is a function of how many insurers and repairers use the platform and how much each pays. One cloud platform connects hundreds of insurers, tens of thousands of repair facilities and thousands of parts suppliers and automakers, which makes each added participant more valuable to the others and leaving costly. Software subscriptions are 95.85% of nine-month 2026 revenue, insurer contracts run about three to five years and repairer contracts about three years, and no customer exceeded 10% of 2025 revenue. For creditors this gives high cash-flow visibility and a low chance of a sudden revenue break, but the franchise depends on staying embedded in United States auto claims.

### Ownership and group structure

The company is a listed Delaware holding company that borrows through its operating subsidiary, CCC Intelligent Solutions Inc., with first-priority liens on that subsidiary's equity and substantially all its assets and guarantees from an intermediate holding company and certain United States subsidiaries. ### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Revenue is predictable and recurring rather than cyclical, with growth coming mainly from existing customers. Software subscriptions grew 10.11% in the first nine months of 2026, about 8% of the 10.55% total growth came from existing customer upgrades and expanded solutions and about 2% from new customers, and software net dollar retention rose from 105% to 108% in a year while gross retention eased from 99% to 98%. The mechanism is switching cost and network effect: customers that depend on the platform for estimates and repair programmes pay more as they add solutions and rarely leave. The implication is revenue visibility that supports debt service, with two caveats: growth leans on upgrades and added solutions sold to existing insurer and repairer customers, and part of revenue is tied to claim volumes, which the company does not quantify.

Weaknesses are reliance on United States auto claims, margins flattered by lower stock compensation, a floating-rate loan with a finite hedge and limited ownership and board visibility. Opportunities that could improve credit quality are artificial-intelligence adoption, expansion into disability and workers' compensation claims, and repayment of the term loan at par in the sale. Threats that could weaken it are debt placed above or beside the term loan by the buyers, insurer in-sourcing, a failed closing with a USD 149.52 million fee, and adverse class-action outcomes.

### Outlook, tailwinds and headwinds

Over the next 12 to 24 months the operating outlook is favourable and the capital structure outlook is open. Management's last full-year guidance, from July 30, 2026, implied revenue growth of 9.56% to 10.12% and adjusted EBITDA growth of 11.23% to 12.60%, and the third quarter beat the top of its quarterly guidance by 0.89% on revenue and 8.78% on adjusted EBITDA, which supports continued growth near 10%. Guidance was then suspended with the merger, so the forward view is no longer management's own. Tailwinds are rising retention, the artificial-intelligence run rate and cost reductions; headwinds are the swap expiry on July 31, 2027, the revolver's springing date in 2028 and the unknown buyer financing, with closing expected in the first quarter of 2027.

### Downside scenario

The most credible downside starts with insurers moving claims workflows to in-house or technology-provider tools, which the company names as competitors. The first operating break would be weaker upsell, shown in net dollar retention falling toward 100% from 108%, followed by contract losses at renewal. As an analyst assumption, not a forecast, those losses take 10% off revenue; with full flow-through to EBITDA and no cost action that would remove USD 113.92 million of earnings, lift gross leverage on the issuer's adjusted measure to 3.42x from 2.62x, and cut free cash flow to USD 197.46 million before any tax effect. If rates also rose 100 bp on the whole loan after the hedge ends, free cash flow would be USD 184.65 million and adjusted cash interest cover 4.35x.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-Q_2026-09-30_ccc-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 34–35; 10-K_2025-12-31_ccc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 25; calculation C13

Resilience appears sufficient to avoid liquidity distress in that scenario: free cash flow still covers scheduled amortisation 14.17x, USD 451.9 million of liquidity remains, no maturity falls due before the revolver's springing date, and management could cut capital spending, which is 5.90% of revenue, and discretionary costs. The primary downside path is therefore not operating failure but a capital structure shock: new buyer debt on top of a revenue setback, at a time when the hedge has ended, would leave less room than the operating numbers alone imply. The observable trigger is the disclosure of the buyer's financing and the treatment of the term loan.

### Overall credit view

Overall, CCC presents a strong business profile supported by recurring subscription revenue, net retention and a network that is hard to replicate, but credit quality is constrained by the pending take-private, a floating-rate loan with a finite hedge, margins flattered by lower stock compensation and concentration in United States auto claims. Cash-flow durability appears strong because free cash flow is 27.33% of revenue and covers scheduled amortisation 23.89x. Refinancing capacity is likely to depend on the treatment of the term loan at closing, the buyers' financing and the revolver's 2028 springing date. The primary downside path is a capital structure shock layered on a modest revenue setback. Further analysis would require the merger agreement, the credit agreement, agency ratings and market prices. Top risk: the take-private resets the capital structure ahead of existing lenders.
