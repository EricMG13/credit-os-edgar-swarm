### Limitations

- Liquidity coverage of principal due to the end of 2027 assumes the revolving facility can be drawn in full, and whether its conditions and the covenants on interest cover, debt to capital and minimum liquidity would allow that in a downturn is not established, so the coverage could be overstated.
- The outlook rests on management guidance and statements about bookings and the 2.75x target, with no independent forecast, peer figures or market prices supplied, so the judgement that earnings will hold and that distributions are affordable cannot be tested against an outside view.
- Cash interest paid is stated only for fiscal years and fiscal 2025 adjusted EBITDA is not stated, so the improvement in cover is shown on interest expense and statement-based EBITDA; on the company's own definitions the trend could differ.
- The documents end on 2026-09-29, so fourth-quarter fuel prices, bookings and repurchases since that date could move the leverage and distribution conclusions in either direction.
- The rating levels are not stated, so the finding that refinancing access is adequate rests on the company's statement of investment grade at two agencies and on filed coupons, and the cost of new borrowing is not established.

### Business and ownership

Carnival is the largest global cruise company, with 94 ships in eight brands split between North America (64% of capacity) and Europe (36%), and a 35.65% share of global cruise capacity at the end of 2025. Tickets are 65.4% of fiscal 2025 revenue and onboard and other spending 34.6%, and guests from the United States are 55.8% of revenue, so the credit rests on North American and European leisure spending. Guests pay a deposit at booking and the balance before sailing, which gives a funding float of customer deposits of USD 7,639 million against cash of USD 1,220 million (6.26x) and a working capital deficit of USD 9.1 billion that is repaid in sailings rather than cash unless sailings are cancelled. For creditors the effect is cash flow that is visible months ahead and spread across brands, but concentrated in one product, and a float that supports liquidity in a normal booking cycle and becomes a claim in a shock.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Costs are largely fixed once capacity is set, so margin depends on yield outrunning cost. In nine months 2026 cruise and tour operating expenses rose 6.27% and selling and administrative expense 7.33% against revenue growth of 4.79%, so operating income fell USD 70 million and the operating margin eased from 18.47% to 17.30%. The company attributes the rise in operating expenses to fuel prices (USD 212 million), capacity (USD 170 million), currency (USD 160 million), the absence of ship-sale gains in 2025 (USD 103 million) and emission allowance costs (USD 54 million), partly offset by lower fuel use per berth day (USD 66 million). Fuel is unhedged, takes 12.56% of operating costs against 11.50% a year earlier, and cost per berth day excluding fuel rose 1.8% in Q3 in constant currency against net yields of 2.4%. Margins can be defended against volume pressure by the cost programme and by slowing capacity, but not against a sustained fuel spike without yield gains, so the cost side is average rather than strong.

### Competitive forces

Rivalry is moderate and supply-led: Carnival and its three main competitors hold about 80% of industry capacity, and in 2025 the industry added 4.27% of capacity against 0.92% for Carnival, so competitors' new ships rather than Carnival's own are the main supply threat to price. Entry is a low threat because few shipyards can build and refit large ships and the capital needed is large, with USD 18.3 billion of Carnival's own ship commitments alone. Substitutes are land-based vacations, which cap price in a downturn when households trade down. Buyers are individuals and travel agents, and commissions, transportation and other costs are 12.41% of revenue, so distribution is a real cost but no buyer has pricing leverage over the group. Suppliers matter most for shipyards, whose limited capacity can delay deliveries and refits, and for fuel, where the company is a price taker; the effect on credit is margin and delivery timing risk, not a threat to liquidity.

### Downside scenario

The realistic downside is a summer-quarter shock that combines regional conflict or hurricane disruption with a fuel price near the guided USD 896 per metric ton. The first break is close-in bookings: in the second quarter of 2026 the company chose price over occupancy in Mediterranean sailings, and a longer episode would cost either price or occupancy in the quarter that earns about 41.92% of annual adjusted EBITDA. Because costs are largely fixed, the company's own sensitivities show how it transmits: in the June outlook a 1% change in net yields was USD 60 million of adjusted net income in the third quarter and in the September outlook USD 49 million in the fourth, and a 10% fuel move USD 59 million. At constant net debt an EBITDA fall of 8.71% returns net leverage to 3.40x and a fall of 27.48% to 4.28x. Liquidity of USD 5,720 million covers principal due to the end of 2027 2.03x, 85% of debt is fixed rate so interest cost would not spike, and the levers are to stop repurchases (USD 929 million in nine months 2026), defer non-newbuild capex and reduce the dividend. The main exposures are refunds on cancelled sailings from the USD 7,639 million deposit float, the minimum liquidity covenant of USD 1.5 billion, and refinancing the USD 3,961 million due in 2028. Resilience appears sufficient to avoid liquidity distress in a shock of the 2026 type, which yields rose through, but a deeper or longer shock is not tested by the documents, and the first observable signs would be deposits below the prior year and net yields below guidance.
