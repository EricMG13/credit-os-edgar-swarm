## Findings

### Bottom line

Genesis Energy is a levered but liquid midstream credit whose offshore pipeline franchise supports debt service, yet deleveraging now depends on offshore volumes recovering in 2027, not on debt repayment.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation F1; calculation F22

Total debt is 5.00x last-twelve-months EBITDA and cash interest cover is 2.41x, so a volume miss reaches coverage quickly. Revolver availability of USD 894.4 million and no maturity before 2029 give time to prove the recovery, while the 2027 covenant step-ups, debt-funded distributions and the 2029 note refinancing are the points to watch.

### Company description and credit linkage

Genesis Energy is a Houston-based midstream partnership that earns fees in three businesses: offshore crude oil and natural gas pipelines in the Gulf of America, a U.S.-flag Jones Act marine fleet, and onshore crude pipelines with sulfur services on the Gulf Coast. Offshore fees are charged per barrel or under capacity arrangements that limit direct exposure to commodity prices, most production handled is under life-of-lease commitments, and 77% of marine revenue was on term contracts in FY2025. That gives the group visible cash flow while producers keep producing, but only about 27.51% of last-twelve-months offshore revenue is fixed or minimum-based for 2027, so visibility fades after 2028. Onshore revenue is largely crude marketing sales that carry thin margins. Debt service capacity is therefore supported by the offshore fee stream, liquidity is preserved by the end of growth spending, and refinancing confidence depends on offshore volumes holding while leverage is above 5x.

### Ownership and group structure

Genesis Energy, L.P. is a listed partnership whose general partner it owns outright. Class B units elect the whole board, one family holds 76.9% of them and can appoint up to three directors, and holders of the preferred units keep a board observer right while they hold enough of the class. The partnership has chosen not to follow listing rules on independent nominating and compensation committees. The notes are guaranteed by the domestic guarantor subsidiaries, while the receivables facility sits in a bankruptcy-remote vehicle outside the guarantor group and its receivables are not available to other creditors. A financial party owns the remaining 36% of CHOPS and the consolidated Independence Hub also carries a noncontrolling interest, so part of consolidated EBITDA leaves the group as distributions to those holders. The credit implication is that control is concentrated, creditors have no governance voice, the 2026 pattern favours equity returns, and the structurally senior claims are the USD 99.5 million receivables facility and minority distributions equal to 11.32% of EBITDA.

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Offshore revenue is volume-driven: fees are fixed per unit of volume under contracts or regulated tariffs, some contracts add capacity-reservation fees, and tiered fees step with cumulative throughput, so earnings follow producers' well performance and the number of rigs that can drill. The mechanism showed in the third quarter, when oil volumes were 0.99% lower and gas volumes 31.98% lower than a year earlier, Shenandoah fell below its minimum commitment and offshore Segment Margin fell 3.50%, although nine-month oil volumes were still 13.26% higher. For credit, offshore revenue is partly predictable through 2028, cyclical with drilling activity, concentrated by field, and exposed to hurricanes; pricing is set by contract and tariff, so growth comes from volume rather than price. Marine revenue is 77% term and 23% spot, utilization is near full and management reports robust spot rates, which supports renewals. Onshore pipeline volumes rose 35.49% in the third quarter, and the sulfur business holds 11 contracts with about two years remaining on average, so onshore is recurring in part but its upside is tied to dislocations. No single customer exceeded 10% of revenue in 2025, although Shell reached 11% in 2024, so customer concentration is moderate and the larger concentration is in producers behind a few fields.

### Cost structure and margin resilience

The offshore cost base is small relative to revenue and, on the evidence of a steady margin, largely fixed, so revenue less operating costs held between 71.05% and 74.87% of revenue from FY2023 to the last twelve months; that margin can be defended under volume pressure only until volumes fall below minimum commitments, after which each lost barrel falls almost entirely to EBITDA. Marine margin after operating costs slipped from 38.87% in FY2024 to 33.52% over the last twelve months; nine-month Segment Margin fell 3.70% on planned dry-docking days, although the third quarter rose 12.16%, and rebilled fuel and other costs that pass through enlarge revenue and costs together, so the percentage understates cash margin. The 23% spot share leaves marine open to rate swings. Onshore margin is 9.85% of revenue after product and operating costs and moves with crude marketing spreads and sulfur prices. Margins are therefore resilient to cost inflation but not to volume loss; the credit implication is that EBITDA is more sensitive to offshore throughput than to any cost lever.

### Capital intensity and cash conversion

### Qualitative downside scenario

The scenario starts with producer underperformance that outlasts management's timetable: Shenandoah stays at its minimum commitment, legacy fields remain curtailed, the Monument wells slip beyond the first half of 2027, and a weak hurricane season adds shut-ins. The first operational break is offshore volume, because offshore margin falls almost one for one once volumes are below the contract floors, and the floors cover only 27.51% of last-twelve-months offshore revenue in 2027. The financial transmission is lower EBITDA with fixed interest: a fall of 12.98% in last-twelve-months EBITDA would put statement-derived leverage at 5.75x and a fall of 16.94% would put cash interest cover at 2.00x, which are reference points at the covenant levels on statement-based measures, not a covenant test, and the 2.15x and 2.25x levels are reached at falls of 10.71% and 6.56%. A fall of 16.94% equals 24.84% of offshore revenue less operating costs, so the scenario requires roughly a quarter of the offshore margin to disappear. The liquidity, leverage and refinancing consequence is that availability under the revolver would depend on covenant compliance and the October 2028 test, so the partnership would need to cut distributions, hold back preferred purchases and refinance the 2029 notes early. Management levers are real: growth capital spending is finished, distributions are discretionary, and USD 894.4 million of revolver availability can bridge a few quarters. Resilience therefore appears sufficient to avoid a liquidity crisis before 2029, but not sufficient to keep leverage falling if volumes stay at the third-quarter level through 2027. The observable triggers are Shenandoah staying at its minimum commitment after the Monument wells connect, guidance missed at the low end, and revolver borrowing used to pay distributions. The primary downside path is a prolonged offshore volume shortfall that lifts leverage and lowers coverage while the covenant levels tighten.
