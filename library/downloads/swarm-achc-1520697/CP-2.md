## Findings

### Bottom line

Acadia is a large, mostly owned behavioral healthcare operator whose credit is constrained by high leverage, near-zero free cash flow and open Medicaid and legal risks, so creditors depend on capital spending staying low and a clean investigation outcome.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A)

Net debt was USD 2.19 billion at 2026-09-30, 4.96x house-normalised EBITDA, because reported EBITDA is negative after a non-cash impairment; liquidity of USD 873.41 million exceeds the USD 535.35 million of principal due through 2028, but the 2028 to 2030 maturities will need refinancing.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation C1; calculation N1; calculation N2

### Key findings

Leverage is the weakest dimension and the earnings base is clouded. Reported EBITDA is negative for 2025 and the last twelve months after USD 1.01 billion of non-cash impairment, so the usable measure is house-normalised: net debt was 3.01x EBITDA at the end of 2024, 5.72x at the end of 2025 and 4.96x at 2026-09-30. The covenant measure is not available, so no headroom is inferred, and the ratio is high enough that an earnings miss would reach refinancing terms before it reached liquidity.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation N3; calculation C4; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 88

Free cash flow is thin and rests on one lever, capital spending. Free cash flow was positive at USD 155.62 million in the nine months of 2026 against an outflow of USD 260.43 million a year earlier, but it is still an outflow of USD 23.86 million over the last twelve months, because 2025 capital spending of USD 571.81 million was cut to 6.40% of revenue from 17.26%. Operating cash flow carries little spare capacity if spending resumes.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation N4; calculation N5; calculation N6; calculation N7

Liquidity is adequate for scheduled debt service but is exposed to non-debt claims. Cash plus the undrawn revolver was USD 873.41 million, covering principal due through 2028 1.63 times, while standby letters of credit rose to USD 15.9 million from USD 1.2 million and settlements, uninsured verdicts and a USD 400.0 million past payment show how fast litigation can draw cash; house-normalised EBITDA covered interest expense 2.91x over the last twelve months.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation N2; calculation C1; calculation C3

Refinancing risk is concentrated in three years. Principal due is USD 494.73 million in 2028, USD 536.10 million in 2029 and USD 745.05 million in 2030, which is 75.04% of the total, with the revolver and term-loan balance falling due together in 2030; the notes are fixed rate and the 2025 refinancing removed any earlier bullet, but market access cannot be judged because no prices or ratings were supplied.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation N8; calculation N9; calculation N10; calculation C2

Business quality is strong on scale and diversification and weak on payor mix. The company operates facilities in 40 states and Puerto Rico, no facility is above 4% of revenue and no state above 14%, and 91% of beds outside treatment centers are in owned property excluded from the credit facility pledge, yet Medicaid supplied 61.8% of nine-month 2026 revenue, so federal and state funding decisions set the revenue base.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 10

Event risk is the main source of downside and part of it is uninsured. A federal criminal investigation and SEC subpoenas cover the acute care line, which was 57.53% of nine-month 2026 revenue, the company has settled matters at USD 400.0 million and USD 179.0 million, and a USD 35.0 million compensatory and USD 70.0 million punitive jury award against an indirect subsidiary is contested.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 18; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 20

Financial policy is neutral to supportive for creditors. No dividend has ever been paid, repurchases have stopped with USD 250.0 million of authority unused, and debt fell USD 147.61 million in 2026, but the unused authority and equity-linked pay for the chief executive leave restart as a monitoring point.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation N11

### Contrary evidence and what would change the view

The strongest evidence against a weak view is the direction of travel: house-normalised net leverage fell from 5.72x to 4.96x, free cash flow turned positive at USD 155.62 million in the nine months, debt fell USD 147.61 million and no behaviour in the filings extracts value from creditors. The strongest evidence for a weaker view is that the improvement comes mainly from cutting capital spending, the earnings base used for leverage is a house measure, and legal outcomes are unquantified.
The view would improve if the investigations close without a material penalty, same-facility growth holds while capital spending stays low for a further year, and the compliance certificate shows covenant room on the agreement's own definition. It would worsen if Medicaid directed-payment caps or eligibility rules reduce revenue per patient day, if an investigation or the contested jury award produces a payment near the scale of the Desert Hills settlement, or if repurchases or growth spending resume before the 2028 notes are refinanced.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C5; calculation N11

### Limitations

- Covenant earnings were not supplied, so the leverage assessment rests on a house-normalised measure and a different definition in the agreement could move it either way.
- No prices, ratings or spreads were supplied, so refinancing capacity is judged from maturity structure and liquidity alone and market access is not assessed.
- The filings contain no management forecast, so the outlook and the downside scenario are causal narratives, not projections, and cannot size a loss.
- No peer set was supplied, so the comparison with typical leveraged-credit issuers is qualitative.

### Company description

Acadia runs 280 behavioral healthcare facilities with about 12,800 beds in 40 states and Puerto Rico through the subsidiaries of a listed holding company. Acute inpatient psychiatric care was 55% of 2025 revenue, specialty treatment 17%, comprehensive treatment centers for opioid use 17% and residential treatment 11%. Demand is clinical and non-discretionary and entry is limited by licensing and certificate-of-need rules, which supports volume visibility, but 72% of revenue comes from Medicare and Medicaid at predetermined rate schedules, so cash-flow visibility rests on government budgets and the company cannot recover cost increases through price.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, pages 10–11; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 16; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 42; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 43

The asset base supports refinancing confidence: 91% of beds outside treatment centers sit in owned property, maintenance capital spending is about 3% of revenue, no facility exceeds 4% of revenue and no state exceeds 14%. These features limit the damage from a single facility or state event but do not offset the payor concentration.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 9; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 11

### Ownership and group structure

No sponsor or controlling holder is identified, so there is no extraction vehicle and no equity backstop; the only investor named, Waud Capital Partners, has a charter carve-out and no stated stake. The wholly-owned U.S. subsidiaries guarantee the credit facility and the three note series, while real property is excluded from the facility pledge, and 19 facilities are held through joint ventures in which partners own 5% to 35% and hold put rights on a change of control over USD 229.88 million of redeemable interests that sit outside debt. The risk for creditors is therefore event and payor risk, not owner behaviour, and governance risk is assessed as Medium because of open investigations and leadership turnover.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation N14; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 21; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 24

### Revenue drivers and pricing power

Revenue is predictable in volume but not in price. Same-facility revenue grew 4.0% in the nine months of 2026, with patient days up 1.2%, admissions up 6.5% and revenue per patient day up 2.8%, so growth is admission and price led while stays shorten. Government payors reimburse on predetermined rate schedules and Medicaid supplied 61.8% of revenue against 53.9% in 2023, which ties pricing power to state budgets and to the federal budget act that caps state directed payments from 2028. Consolidated revenue grew 5.04% in 2025 and 3.96% in the nine months of 2026, and the shift of mix toward acute inpatient care increases dependence on the line under investigation.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); Financial foundation (CP-1); 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 38; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 43

### Cost structure and margin resilience

Margins have compressed and the cost base is largely fixed. Salaries, wages and benefits were 55.27% of nine-month 2026 revenue, the company reports a shortage of nurses and counselors that forces wage increases and costlier contract labor, and other operating expenses, mainly purchased services, insurance and provider taxes, rose to 16.7% of 2025 revenue from 14.0% and were 16.3% in the nine months of 2026 with a USD 28.6 million unfavourable liability adjustment. House-normalised EBITDA margin fell to 12.48% in 2025 from 19.82% in 2024 and was 12.94% for the last twelve months, so margins cannot be defended under volume pressure without cost action, and cost flexibility sits mainly in growth capital spending rather than operating costs.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation C4; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 69; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 87; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 44

### Capital intensity and FCF conversion

EBITDA converts to free cash flow only when growth spending is cut. Maintenance capital spending is about 3% of revenue, but total spending was 17.26% of 2025 revenue and 6.40% in the nine months of 2026; operating cash flow of USD 321.48 million in the nine months funded USD 165.86 million of spending and a revolver paydown. Cash paid for interest was USD 124.66 million in 2025 against interest expense, net of USD 138.86 million, government investigation costs fell to USD 38.16 million in the nine months of 2026 from USD 123.27 million, and last-twelve-month interest cover on an accrual basis was 2.91x, so free cash flow after interest remains small.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation N12; calculation N13; calculation C3; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 29

### Porter's five forces

Rivalry is moderate and local: competitors include non-profit and government-owned providers with tax advantages and independent operators with strong reputations, which can reduce patient volume if they recruit staff or win payor contracts better. Entry is limited by licensing, accreditation and certificate-of-need approval, which protects existing beds. Substitutes matter through lower-cost settings and shorter stays, with same-facility length of stay down 5.0% in the nine months of 2026. Buyer power is high because government programs and managed care set rates, and supplier power sits mainly in scarce nurses, counselors and psychiatrists. Together these forces cap pricing and keep labor cost high, which limits margin durability.
Basis: 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 16; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 43; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 67; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 69; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 38

### PEST

Political and regulatory factors are a primary credit driver. The federal budget act reduces federal Medicaid spending, bars new provider taxes, caps state directed payments and requires work or community engagement rules by 2026-12-31 with six-monthly eligibility checks, and the company also faces criminal and securities investigations; economic factors matter through loss of insurance coverage after the end of enhanced marketplace subsidies. Social and technological factors do not appear to be primary credit drivers on the materials supplied, apart from cybersecurity incidents the company discloses it has experienced.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 36–37; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 18; 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 63

### SWOT

Strengths are scale across 40 states, owned real estate, no facility above 4% of revenue, falling capital intensity and no extraction by owners. Weaknesses are leverage that is high on a house-normalised basis, negative reported EBITDA, thin free cash flow, a labor-heavy cost base and Medicaid dependence. Opportunities that could improve credit quality are joint venture growth funded by partners, deleveraging from lower spending and closing the investigations. Threats are Medicaid funding changes, penalties or uninsured verdicts, wage inflation and a refinancing wall in 2028 to 2030.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); Financial foundation (CP-1)

### Financial profile and credit quality

On a ratings-style lens the financial profile is weak to average. Scale and market position are strong, competitive advantage and business diversification are average because service lines are spread but payors are concentrated, cost and capex flexibility is average because spending can be cut but labor cannot, margin stability and free cash flow stability are weak, the ability to refinance is average with market access not assessable, liquidity is average to strong, and financial policy and governance are average. The main supports are scale, owned real estate, liquidity and deleveraging since 2025; the main constraints are leverage on an unmeasured covenant basis, thin free cash flow and event risk; the trend is improving from the 2025 trough; missing datapoints are covenant earnings, market pricing and investigation outcomes.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C1; calculation C4

### Outlook, tailwinds and headwinds

The next 12 to 24 months are decided by policy and legal dates. Medicaid work and community engagement rules must be in place by 2026-12-31, the final approval hearing for the derivative settlement is set for 2026-12-10, trial of the sixth Desert Hills lawsuit is set for 2027-04-19, and the 5.500% notes due 2028-07-01 become current in July 2027. Tailwinds are demand for behavioral care, joint venture capacity funded by partners and lower investigation costs; headwinds are Medicaid funding limits, labor cost, insurance cost and leadership transition. Over three to five years the federal cap on state directed payments steps down from 2028 and the 2028, 2029 and 2030 maturities need refinancing, so credit quality depends on holding capital spending low while rates and volumes hold.
Basis: 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, pages 36–37; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 15; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 17; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 23; Business & governance (CP-1A); Financial foundation (CP-1)

### Downside scenario

The initiating condition is a Medicaid rate or eligibility reduction, such as a state directed payment cap, arriving in the same period as an enforcement outcome. The first operational break is revenue per patient day, because government rate schedules do not rise with cost while salaries, wages and benefits are 55.27% of revenue, so margin falls faster than revenue. The financial transmission is lower house-normalised EBITDA against fixed debt, which lifts leverage above the 4.96x reached at 2026-09-30, while a settlement of the scale of the USD 400.0 million paid in 2024 would equal 45.80% of current liquidity of USD 873.41 million. The liquidity consequence is manageable through 2028 because liquidity covers scheduled principal 1.63 times, but the refinancing consequence is severe if leverage is high when the 2028 notes must be refinanced. Management levers are growth capital spending, held-for-sale property, the paused repurchase program and revolver availability; the observable triggers are same-facility revenue per patient day, the Medicaid share of revenue and any investigation development. Resilience appears sufficient to avoid liquidity distress through 2028 absent a combined shock.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); calculation C1; calculation C5; calculation N2

### Overall credit view

Overall, Acadia presents an adequate business profile supported by scale across 40 states, owned real estate and non-discretionary demand, but credit quality is constrained by leverage that is high on a house-normalised basis, negative reported EBITDA, thin free cash flow and open Medicaid and legal risks. Cash-flow durability appears weak to adequate because it depends on capital spending staying low. Refinancing capacity is likely to depend on market access that is not assessable here and on leverage at the time the 2028 notes mature. The primary downside path is a Medicaid payment cut combined with a legal cash call while leverage is high. Further analysis would require the compliance certificate and covenant definitions, market prices and ratings, and investigation outcomes. Completed. Top risk: Medicaid payment reductions combined with a large uninsured legal payment.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); Business & governance (CP-1A); Source readiness (CP-0)

### Strengths and weaknesses

Scale, diversification and owned real estate are the main strengths. The company operates 280 facilities with no facility above 4% of revenue and no state above 14%, and 91% of beds outside treatment centers are owned property excluded from the facility pledge; this spreads facility and state risk and gives lenders an unencumbered asset pool, which supports recovery relevance and refinancing confidence.
Basis: Business & governance (CP-1A); 10-K_2025-12-31_achc_achc-20251231.htm.jsonl, page 9; 10-Q_2026-09-30_achc_achc-20260930.htm.jsonl, page 21

Falling capital intensity and a repaid revolver are the second strength. Capital spending fell to 6.40% of revenue in the nine months of 2026 from 17.26% in 2025 and debt fell by USD 147.61 million in 2026, which turned nine-month free cash flow positive and eased house-normalised net leverage from 5.72x to 4.96x.
Basis: Financial foundation (CP-1); calculation N11

Creditor-neutral financial policy is the third strength. No dividend has ever been paid and repurchases stopped, so no value is extracted from creditors, although the unused authority remains.

Medicaid dependence is the second weakness. Medicaid supplied 61.8% of nine-month 2026 revenue and federal changes cap state directed payments from 2028, so rate cuts would reach a labor-heavy cost base.

Legal and regulatory event risk is the third weakness. The criminal investigation of the acute care line, a contested jury award and insurance exhaustion for some years can turn into cash calls ahead of debt reduction.
